Active vs. Passive: Who Did It?
The grammar of agency — and how to assign (or hide) blame
🎯 WHAT THIS LESSON IS FOR
This whole module asks who is responsible? — and grammar has a tool that points right at that question. Voice decides whether a sentence names the doer of an action or hides them. Learning to control it makes your writing clearer and your arguments sharper, and it lets you see when a writer (or a politician, or a character) is quietly dodging blame.
✏️ PART 1 · GRAMMAR: TWO VOICES

Notice it first. Compare:

Active: “Victor abandoned the creature.” — subject (Victor) does the action.
Passive: “The creature was abandoned.” — subject (the creature) receives it; the doer can vanish.

Name it. In the active voice, the subject performs the verb. In the passive voice, the subject is acted upon, and the real doer is either tucked into a “by…” phrase or dropped entirely. Passive is built from a form of to be + a past participle (was made, is created, were killed).

The rule of thumb. Prefer the active voice — it’s clearer, shorter, and names who is responsible. Use the passive on purpose, when the doer is genuinely unknown (“the body was found”) or when you want to spotlight the receiver. Watch for the passive that hides a doer who should be named: “mistakes were made” is how people avoid saying “I made a mistake.”

⚡ USE IT · ACTIVE OR PASSIVE?
Pick one answer per row; you’ll get an instant note.
1. “The creature was rejected by everyone he met.”
2. “Victor destroyed the half-finished creature.”
3. Which sentence hides who is responsible?

In your own analytical writing, default to active voice when you’re assigning responsibility: “Shelley shows…,” “Victor chooses…,” “the creature decides…” It makes your claims land.

📚 PART 2 · VOCABULARY FROM THE READING

Five words from this week’s chapters, in the sense they carry there.

concede (verb)
to grant or yield, often reluctantly. The creature demands a right Victor “must not refuse to concede.”
malicious (adjective)
intending harm. “I am malicious because I am miserable,” he says.
abhorrence (noun)
deep disgust or hatred. Victor fears the mate might feel “greater abhorrence” for the creature.
propagate (verb)
to multiply or spread. Victor dreads a “race of devils” that would be propagated on the earth.
inexorable (adjective)
impossible to stop or move; relentless. “Leave me; I am inexorable,” Victor tells the creature.
requite (verb)
to return or repay (a feeling or act). His affection was “requited by detestation and scorn.”
✏️ WRITTEN PRACTICE · COPIA

Start from this sentence:

“The creature’s affection was requited with abhorrence, and he grew malicious.”

In your reading notes, rewrite this sentence two ways: (1) put the whole thing into the active voice, naming who does each action; (2) rewrite it about a different moment in the novel. Use at least two of this week’s vocabulary words across your sentences.

💡 CARRY IT INTO YOUR WORK

This week’s Quick Write asks who was right and who is to blame — questions of agency. Write those claims in the active voice so it’s always clear who did what: “Victor breaks his promise,” not “the promise was broken.” And when you read, notice who reaches for the passive to slip out from under responsibility.
