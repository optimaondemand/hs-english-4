📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Almost everyone says they want to be happy. Aristotle asks the harder question underneath: what is happiness for?
Aristotle was Plato’s student, but he works differently. Instead of a story like the Cave, he reasons step by step. He notices that we choose most things for the sake of something else — money for what it buys, exercise for health — and asks whether there is anything we choose only for itself. His name for that one final goal is eudaimonia, usually translated “happiness,” though it means something closer to a whole life lived well.

▶  Read the passage — on happiness (Nicomachean Ethics, Book I)

Now that which is an object of pursuit in itself we call more final than that which is so with a view to something else… and so by the term “absolutely final,” we denote that which is an object of choice always in itself, and never with a view to any other.

And of this nature Happiness is mostly thought to be, for this we choose always for its own sake, and never with a view to anything further: whereas honour, pleasure, intellect… we choose… also with a view to happiness, conceiving that through their instrumentality we shall be happy: but no man chooses happiness with a view to them, nor in fact with a view to any other thing whatsoever.

… we define that to be self-sufficient “which taken alone makes life choice-worthy, and to be in want of nothing;” now of such kind we think Happiness to be… So then Happiness is manifestly something final and self-sufficient, being the end of all things which are and may be done.

If then the work of Man is a working of the soul… the Good of Man comes to be “a working of the Soul in the way of Excellence,” or, if Excellence admits of degrees, in the way of the best and most perfect Excellence. And we must add, in a complete life: for as it is not one swallow or one fine day that makes a spring, so it is not one day or a short time that makes a man blessed and happy.

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book I (D. P. Chase translation). Public domain English text. Excerpts.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Aristotle (384–322 BCE) studied for about twenty years under Plato in Athens and later tutored the young Alexander the Great. The Nicomachean Ethics is thought to come from his lectures — it reads like careful, working-through argument rather than polished prose, and is named for Nicomachus, probably his son. Where Plato pointed beyond the everyday world toward eternal truths, Aristotle keeps his eye on this life: what an actual good human life consists of, and how a person builds one.
📚 PART 1 · THE GOAL BEHIND THE GOALS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of something you want this year. Now ask why you want it — and keep asking “why” to each answer. Where does the chain seem to stop? (No wrong answer.)

Aristotle starts from a plain observation: almost everything we pursue, we pursue for the sake of something else. You study for a grade, the grade for a diploma, the diploma for a job, the job for a life you want. But that chain cannot run forever, or nothing would ever be worth wanting. So there must be at least one thing we choose only for itself and never as a means to something else. That, he argues, is what people are really naming when they say happiness. It is “final” because we never want it in order to get something further, and “self-sufficient” because, as he puts it, it is what “taken alone makes life choice-worthy, and to be in want of nothing.” Notice he is not saying happiness is a good mood. He is saying it is the target the whole of a life points toward.

📚 PART 2 · HAPPINESS AS AN ACTIVITY, NOT A FEELING

Here Aristotle makes a turn that still surprises readers. He asks what the “work” or characteristic activity of a human being is — the thing a person does that nothing else does — and answers that it is the soul reasoning and acting well. So the good life is not a feeling that happens to you; it is an activity you perform: “a working of the Soul in the way of Excellence.” A harpist’s job is to play the harp, a good harpist’s job is to play it well; a human being’s job is to live, and a good human being’s job is to live well, reasoning and choosing well over time. And he adds a quiet, demanding condition: this has to be measured across “a complete life,” because “it is not one swallow or one fine day that makes a spring.” One good afternoon, or one good year, does not settle whether a life went well. That is a high bar — and it sets up everything in the next two lessons, where Aristotle explains how a person actually becomes the kind of soul that works well.

💡 WHERE THIS CONNECTS

Set Aristotle’s “final good” next to the others you have read. The Gita urged acting without attachment to the result; the Buddhist parables traced suffering to craving; Confucius pointed to the cultivated person; Plato pointed to the climb toward truth. Aristotle gives this whole module its title question a direct, this-worldly answer: a good life is the soul reasoning and acting well, all the way through.
