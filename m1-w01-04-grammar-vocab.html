Five Words, and the Semicolon
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two tools you can use right away: five words worth keeping from this week’s reading, and the semicolon — a single punctuation mark that fixes one of the most common errors in student essays.
First you will add five words to your vocabulary and practice using one of them in your own sentence. Then you will learn when to use a semicolon, how it differs from a comma, and how it fixes the comma splice.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define five words from the reading and use one of them in a sentence of my own.
2
I will be able to join two related sentences with a semicolon and fix a comma splice three different ways.
📚 PART 1 · VOCABULARY FROM THE READING

These five words appear in this week’s passages. They are useful well beyond the Gita — you will meet them in other reading and can use them in your own writing. Tap each card to check the definition, then add all five to the Vocabulary Log in your Learning Log.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE DEFINITION
equable (adj.)
calm and even; not thrown off by good or bad outcomes. “equable / In good or evil”
ineffable (adj.)
too great or sacred to be put into words. “Invisible, ineffable”
mutable (adj.)
able to change; changeable. “’Tis brief and mutable!”
contemn (v.)
to treat as worthless; to scorn. (Not the same as condemn.) “Contemning gain and merit”
wrought (v./adj.)
made, worked, or done (an older past form of work). “the bondage of wrought deeds”
✏️ WRITE IT · COPIA
Choose one of the five words. Find the line it appears in within this week’s reading, and rewrite that line in your own words, keeping the meaning but using your own phrasing. Put it in your Vocabulary Log. (This is called copia — practicing a word by reworking a real sentence that uses it. You’ll do it every week.)
📚 PART 2 · THE SEMICOLON

A semicolon ( ; ) joins two complete sentences that are closely related in meaning. By “complete sentence” we mean a group of words that could stand on its own — it has a subject and a verb and expresses a full thought (grammar calls this an independent clause). This week’s reading uses semicolons constantly to link related statements. You already read one:

📝 ON THE BOARD
“Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never…”
Two complete statements about the spirit, closely linked — so a semicolon joins them.

The most common reason to reach for a semicolon is to fix a comma splice — the error of joining two complete sentences with only a comma. Here is the error, and the three standard ways to fix it:

⚠️ THE COMMA SPLICE
Incorrect: The body dies, the soul does not.
Two complete sentences joined by only a comma. A comma is too weak to do that job.
Fix 1 — period: The body dies. The soul does not.
Fix 2 — semicolon: The body dies; the soul does not.
Fix 3 — comma + joining word: The body dies, but the soul does not.

All three are correct. Choose the semicolon when the two ideas are tightly connected and you want to show that link without starting a new sentence or adding a word like but or and. One caution: do not use a semicolon to attach a fragment (something that is not a complete sentence). “The body dies; because it is mortal” is wrong, because “because it is mortal” cannot stand alone.

⚡ TRY IT · PUNCTUATION CHECK
Pick one answer per row; you’ll get an instant note.
1. Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
2. Which one contains a comma splice (the error)?
3. Best single-mark fix for: “Krishna gives a comparison, it makes the idea clear.”
4. When should you not use a semicolon?
💡 WHY THIS IS WORTH KNOWING

The comma splice is one of the most frequently marked errors in high-school and college essays, and the semicolon is the quickest way to fix it. It also gives you a way to show that two ideas belong together without breaking them into short, choppy sentences. Watch for a chance to use one correctly in this week’s Quick Write.
