We Become What We Repeatedly Do
Virtue as habit, and the idea of the mean
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday Aristotle said the good life is the soul working well. Today he answers the obvious follow-up: how does a soul get to work well?
His answer is unglamorous and powerful: by practice. You are not born just or brave or self-controlled — you become those things by repeatedly doing just, brave, and self-controlled acts, the same way you learn an instrument by playing it. And he gives a rule for what those right acts look like: they sit in a mean, a balance point between doing too much and doing too little.

▶  Read the passage — on habit and the mean (Nicomachean Ethics, Book II)

… what we have to make when we have learned how, these we learn how to make by making: men come to be builders, for instance, by building; harp-players, by playing on the harp: exactly so, by doing just actions we come to be just; by doing the actions of self-mastery we come to be perfected in self-mastery; and by doing brave actions brave.

And to the truth of this testimony is borne by what takes place in communities: because the law-givers make the individual members good men by habituation…

In all quantity… one may take the greater part, the less, or the exactly equal… and the exactly equal is a mean between excess and defect. Now by the mean… relatively to ourselves, [I denote] that which is neither too much nor too little for the particular individual…

… suppose ten [portions of food] is too much and two too little… it does not follow… that the trainer will order his man six; because for the person who is to take it this also may be too much or too little: for Milo it would be too little, but for a man just commencing his athletic exercises too much… So then it seems every one possessed of skill avoids excess and defect, but seeks for and chooses the mean, not the absolute but the relative.

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II (D. P. Chase translation). Public domain English text. Excerpts. (Milo of Croton was a famously strong ancient wrestler.)
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Aristotle was, among many other things, a working biologist who dissected animals and classified hundreds of species. That habit of mind shows here: instead of asking what goodness is in the abstract, he watches how people actually become skilled at anything — a craft, a sport, an instrument — and reasons that becoming good must work the same way. His Greek word for these settled good states of character, hexis, is the root of our word for a fixed disposition; in this translation it appears as a state built by “habituation.”
📚 PART 1 · CHARACTER IS BUILT, NOT BORN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of one thing you are genuinely good at. How did you get good at it? (No wrong answer.)

Aristotle’s claim is that the virtues are skills of character, learned the way any skill is learned: “by doing just actions we come to be just; by doing the actions of self-mastery we come to be perfected in self-mastery; and by doing brave actions brave.” You do not first become brave and then act bravely — you become brave by doing brave things, the way you become a builder by building. This flips a common assumption. People often treat character as something you either have or don’t. Aristotle treats it as something you construct, one act at a time, until acting well becomes second nature. That is hopeful — anyone can start — but also demanding, because it means your small repeated choices are quietly making you into someone.

📚 PART 2 · THE MEAN: NEITHER TOO MUCH NOR TOO LITTLE

But practice toward what? Aristotle’s answer is the idea he is most famous for: each virtue is a mean — a balance point — between two faults, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage sits between recklessness (too much) and cowardice (too little). Generosity sits between wastefulness and stinginess. The crucial detail is that the mean is “not the absolute but the relative.” It is not a fixed midpoint like the number six between two and ten. He uses a trainer feeding an athlete: the right amount of food is not automatically the middle number, because “for Milo it would be too little, but for a man just commencing his athletic exercises too much.” The right action depends on who you are, the situation, and the moment. That makes Aristotle’s ethics less like a rulebook and more like good judgment trained over time — which is exactly why he made habit the foundation in Part 1. You build the judgment by practicing it.

💡 WHERE THIS CONNECTS

Aristotle’s “we become just by doing just acts” rhymes with several earlier readings. Confucius, too, saw the good person as cultivated by steady practice, not born finished. The Buddhist parables treated craving as a habit to be unlearned. Hold these together: more than one tradition in this module locates the good life in what you repeatedly do, not in what you happen to feel. In the final lesson, Aristotle asks which activity, practiced well, is the very highest a human can reach.
