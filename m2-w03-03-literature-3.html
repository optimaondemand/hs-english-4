📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
After months of love from a distance, the creature finally tries to be known — and the attempt destroys him.
He waits until the blind old man, De Lacey, is alone — the one person who cannot judge him by his face — and speaks to him. For a moment, kindness. Then the family returns, sees him, and Felix beats him and drives him out. This is the hinge of the whole novel: the exact point where a being who “was benevolent and good” is turned, by rejection, toward “rage and revenge.” Today you watch a person become a monster — and decide who is responsible.

📖 THE READING

The creature has just spoken with the blind De Lacey when the family returns. Read before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — the rejection, and the turn (Chapters 15–16)
The family returns:

“At that instant the cottage door was opened, and Felix, Safie, and Agatha entered. Who can describe their horror and consternation on beholding me? Agatha fainted, and Safie… rushed out of the cottage. Felix darted forward, and… tore me from his father… in a transport of fury, he dashed me to the ground and struck me violently with a stick. I could have torn him limb from limb, as the lion rends the antelope. But my heart sank within me as with bitter sickness, and I refrained.”

The turn (Chapter 16):

“Cursed, cursed creator! Why did I live?… my feelings were those of rage and revenge… there was none among the myriads of men that existed who would pity or assist me; and should I feel kindness towards my enemies? No… I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me.”

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapters 15–16.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · JUDGED BY THE SURFACE
Shelley makes the one person who is kind to the creature a blind man — and the detail is doing heavy thematic work. De Lacey, who hears the creature’s gentle, intelligent speech but cannot see his body, treats him as a fellow human. Everyone who can see him reacts with horror before he says a word. The novel is built around this gap between what the creature is (thoughtful, longing, capable of good) and how he is seen (a horror to be driven off). It is one of literature’s sharpest studies of how appearance overrides character — how a being can be condemned entirely on the surface, never given the chance to be known.
📚 PART 1 · THE ONE WHO COULDN'T SEE HIM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever been judged on how you looked or seemed — before anyone gave you a chance to show who you actually are? (No wrong answer.)

The creature’s plan is heartbreakingly smart. He has learned that his face is the problem, so he waits to approach De Lacey when the old man is alone and blind — the one person who can judge him by his words and heart instead of his appearance. And it works, for a few minutes: the old man listens kindly. Then the others come home and see him, and the moment shatters. “Who can describe their horror… on beholding me?” Felix attacks. And here is the detail that proves who the creature still is at this point: he is strong enough to kill — “I could have torn him limb from limb, as the lion rends the antelope” — but “my heart sank… and I refrained.” Even being beaten by the family he loved and served, he will not strike back. The goodness is still there. The novel is making sure we cannot miss it, right up to the edge of the change.

📚 PART 2 · THE HINGE: WHO MAKES A MONSTER?

Then comes the turn the entire novel has been building toward. Driven out, having lost the one family he loved, the creature’s feelings become “those of rage and revenge.” He reasons his way there: “there was none among the myriads of men… who would pity or assist me; and should I feel kindness towards my enemies? No.” He concludes, echoing Milton’s Satan again, “I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me.” Read that sequence carefully, because it is an argument, not just an outburst. The creature began benevolent (Lesson 3.1). He educated himself and longed to belong (Lesson 3.2). He made one careful, peaceful attempt to be accepted — and was met with violence for his appearance alone. Then he turns. Shelley has constructed the most important question of the book and handed it to you: when a being is made gentle, then taught by relentless cruelty that gentleness earns him nothing, who is responsible for what he becomes — the creature, the family who beat him, or the creator who abandoned him to all of it? There is no easy answer, and that difficulty is exactly the point. A mature reader holds the creature’s coming crimes and his genuine victimhood in view at the same time.
