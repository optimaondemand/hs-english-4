📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The creature finishes his story and makes one request — the only thing, he says, that could turn him back toward good.
He asks Victor to make him a companion: a female creature like himself, so that he will not be utterly alone. It is the logical end of everything he has told us — he was driven to rage by isolation, so the cure is connection. But he also makes clear what happens if Victor refuses. Today you weigh the creature’s argument: is it a fair request, a threat, or both at once?

📖 THE READING

The creature has finished his story and now makes his demand. Read before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — the demand, and the reasoning (Chapter 17)

“You must create a female for me with whom I can live in the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being. This you alone can do, and I demand it of you as a right which you must not refuse to concede.”

When Victor refuses, the creature reasons:

“I am malicious because I am miserable. Am I not shunned and hated by all mankind?… Let him live with me in the interchange of kindness, and instead of injury I would bestow every benefit upon him with tears of gratitude at his acceptance. But that cannot be… if I cannot inspire love, I will cause fear, and chiefly towards you my arch-enemy…”

“If any being felt emotions of benevolence towards me, I should return them a hundred and a hundredfold… My vices are the children of a forced solitude that I abhor, and my virtues will necessarily arise when I live in communion with an equal.”

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapter 17. (After this exchange, Victor reluctantly agrees to make the companion.)
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · “SYMPATHY”
When the creature asks for “the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being,” he is using a word that carried great weight in Shelley’s time. For 18th- and early-19th-century thinkers, sympathy meant more than pity: it was the capacity to feel with another person, and many philosophers (such as Adam Smith) argued that this fellow-feeling is the very foundation of morality — that we become good by entering into others’ experience. The creature’s claim is built on exactly this idea: cut off from all sympathy, he cannot be good; given one being to share feeling with, he could be. He is not asking for a luxury. By the moral theory of his own era, he is asking for the precondition of having a moral life at all.
📚 PART 1 · THE ARGUMENT FOR A COMPANION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Do you think a person cut off from all companionship — never befriended, never loved — could stay kind and good? (No wrong answer.)

The creature’s request is precise and self-aware. He does not ask to rejoin human society — he knows “the human senses are insurmountable barriers” to that. He asks only for one being like himself, so he can live “in the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being.” And he offers a diagnosis of his own wickedness: “I am malicious because I am miserable.” His vices, he argues, are “the children of a forced solitude,” and “my virtues will necessarily arise when I live in communion with an equal.” This is a genuine moral argument, and it lands hard because everything we read last week supports it — we watched him be benevolent until cruelty broke him. He is asking Victor to remove the cause of his evil rather than just punish its effects.

📚 PART 2 · A PLEA WRAPPED AROUND A THREAT

But read the request again and notice its other half. The same speech that promises “tears of gratitude” also warns: “if I cannot inspire love, I will cause fear, and chiefly towards you my arch-enemy.” He even calls the companion a “right which you must not refuse to concede” — a demand, not a wish. So which is it? A heartbreaking plea from a lonely being, or extortion backed by a threat of violence? Shelley refuses to let it be simple. It is both, and the doubleness is the point. The creature is sincere that love would make him good — and he is also dangerous, and saying so. This is what makes Victor’s decision genuinely hard: to refuse may doom an innocent to eternal misery and provoke more murder; to agree may unleash a second creature, or a whole race of them, on the world. Hold that dilemma; next lesson, Victor makes his choice, and it is not the one he first agrees to here.
