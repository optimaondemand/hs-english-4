📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The creature promised, “I shall be with you on your wedding-night.” Victor heard a threat against himself.
On his wedding night, Victor arms himself and waits for the creature to come for him — and leaves Elizabeth alone. The mistake is fatal. The creature was never coming for Victor; he comes, as he always said he would, for what Victor loves. Today you watch Shelley spring a trap she set chapters ago, and see how Victor’s self-absorption — reading every threat as being about him — costs Elizabeth her life.

📖 THE READING

Victor describes the night of his wedding to Elizabeth. Read before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — the misreading, and Elizabeth (Chapters 22–23)
Victor reads the vow as a threat to himself:

… the words rang in my ears—“I will be with you on your wedding-night!”… on that night would the dæmon employ every art to destroy me and tear me from the glimpse of happiness which promised partly to console my sufferings.

What he finds instead:

… what was the destruction of the best hope and the purest creature on earth? She was there, lifeless and inanimate, thrown across the bed, her head hanging down and her pale and distorted features half covered by her hair… her bloodless arms and relaxed form flung by the murderer on its bridal bier.

… A grin was on the face of the monster; he seemed to jeer, as with his fiendish finger he pointed towards the corpse of my wife.

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapters 22–23.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · DRAMATIC IRONY
Dramatic irony is when the reader understands something a character does not. By this point, we can see what Victor cannot: the creature has murdered William and framed Justine, then killed Clerval — always striking at Victor’s loved ones, never at Victor himself. So when the creature vows to be there “on your wedding-night,” the reader feels the dread the warning should produce. Victor, blind to the pattern, prepares to defend his own life. Shelley uses the gap between what we know and what Victor sees to make Elizabeth’s death almost unbearable — and to indict the very flaw that causes it.
📚 PART 1 · THE MISREADING
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Reading the creature’s vow now, knowing the whole story, who did you think he meant to harm on the wedding night? (No wrong answer.)

Victor hears “I will be with you on your wedding-night” and concludes “on that night would the dæmon… destroy me.” Read that “me” carefully — it is the same self-centeredness that has shaped him all along. He made the creature for his own glory; he hid the truth to protect his own reputation while Justine hanged; and now, warned of an attack on his wedding night, he can imagine only that he is the target. So he sends Elizabeth to their room alone and patrols for his own attacker. The creature, who has told Victor exactly what he intends — to make him as alone as he himself is — simply keeps his word. Victor’s flaw is not stupidity; it is a self-focus so deep he cannot picture a world where he is not the center, even of his enemy’s hatred.

📚 PART 2 · THE PUREST CREATURE

Then the discovery: Elizabeth “lifeless and inanimate, thrown across the bed… flung by the murderer on its bridal bier.” Shelley calls her “the best hope and the purest creature on earth” — and the choice of victim completes the creature’s revenge. Elizabeth is the love that was supposed to “console” Victor’s sufferings, the future he was building. The creature destroys her precisely because she is the companion Victor would have, while he was denied any companion at all. And note the final cruelty: the creature lingers at the window “to jeer,” pointing “towards the corpse of my wife.” He wants Victor to see — to understand that this is the answer to the destroyed promise of Chapter 20. The bargain refused has become the bargain repaid. Every loved one Victor has lost — William, Justine, Clerval, now Elizabeth — is a step in the creature giving his maker the exact gift his maker gave him: total isolation.
