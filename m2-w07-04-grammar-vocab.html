The Comma That Sets the Stage
Commas after introductory phrases and clauses
🎯 WHAT THIS LESSON IS FOR
The last grammar lesson of the module targets one of the most common comma errors there is — and one that matters as you polish your project this week. When a sentence opens with a phrase or a dependent clause before the main idea, a comma marks where the lead-in ends and the sentence proper begins. Get this right and your sentences read clean; miss it and readers stumble.
✏️ PART 1 · GRAMMAR: COMMA AFTER THE LEAD-IN

The rule. When a sentence begins with an introductory element — a phrase or a dependent clause that sets up the main clause — follow it with a comma:

Dependent clause: Although the creature began gentle, the world made him a fiend.
Long phrase: In the genealogy of morals, Nietzsche traces where “good” came from.
Transition: Finally, Victor admits his guilt.

Two notes: (1) the comma goes after the whole lead-in, where the main clause begins — not in the middle of it. (2) With a very short opener you can sometimes drop the comma (“Soon he fled”), but adding it is never wrong, and with anything longer it’s required. When in doubt, set the stage, then comma.

📚 PART 2 · VOCABULARY FROM THE READING

Five words from this week’s Nietzsche.

genealogy (noun)
a tracing of origins and descent. Nietzsche writes a “genealogy” of moral ideas.
aristocratic (adjective)
of the noble or ruling class. “Aristocratic morality” affirms its own strength as “good.”
resentment (noun)
bitter ill-will at a perceived wrong. Slave morality, Nietzsche says, is born of resentment.
antithesis (noun)
a direct opposite. “Good and bad” began as an antithesis between noble and low.
affirmation (noun)
a positive assertion; a “yes.” Master morality begins in a “triumphant affirmation” of itself.
preponderance (noun)
superiority in number or force; the upper hand. One value, Nietzsche says, has long held the preponderance.
✏️ WRITTEN PRACTICE · COPIA

Start from this sentence:

“In Nietzsche’s genealogy, the antithesis of good and bad began as an aristocratic affirmation, and resentment later reversed it.”

In your notes, write two sentences of your own, each opening with an introductory phrase or clause, correctly punctuated — one about Nietzsche, one about Frankenstein. Use at least two of this week’s vocabulary words.

💡 CARRY IT INTO YOUR PROJECT

As you revise and polish over the next two weeks, the introductory comma is a quick, high-value fix to scan for — it’s one of the errors a reader notices most. Read each sentence that opens with a lead-in and make sure the comma marks where the real sentence begins.
