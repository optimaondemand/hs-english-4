📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Here is a question every tradition in this module has circled: why be good at all? Plato puts it as sharply as anyone ever has — with a story about a magic ring.
In the Republic, a young man named Glaucon tells the story of a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible. As you read, notice that Glaucon is not telling it to praise injustice — he is daring Socrates to prove that being just is worth it even when you could get away with anything.

▶  Read the passage — the Ring of Gyges (Republic, Book II)

According to the tradition, Gyges was a shepherd in the service of the king of Lydia; there was a great storm, and an earthquake made an opening in the earth at the place where he was feeding his flock. Amazed at the sight, he descended into the opening, where… he beheld a hollow brazen horse, having doors, at which he stooping and looking in saw a dead body… having nothing on but a gold ring; this he took from the finger of the dead and reascended.

… as he was sitting among [the shepherds] he chanced to turn the collet of the ring inside his hand, when instantly he became invisible to the rest of the company… he made several trials of the ring, and always with the same result—when he turned the collet inwards he became invisible, when outwards he reappeared. Whereupon he contrived to be chosen one of the messengers who were sent to the court; where… he seduced the queen, and with her help conspired against the king and slew him, and took the kingdom.

Suppose now that there were two such magic rings, and the just put on one of them and the unjust the other; no man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice. No man would keep his hands off what was not his own when he could safely take what he liked…, or kill or release from prison whom he would, and in all respects be like a God among men. Then the actions of the just would be as the actions of the unjust; they would both come at last to the same point.

And this we may truly affirm to be a great proof that a man is just, not willingly or because he thinks that justice is any good to him individually, but of necessity, for wherever any one thinks that he can safely be unjust, there he is unjust.

Plato, Republic, Book II. Public domain English text. Excerpt.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Plato (about 428–348 BCE) was an Athenian philosopher and a student of Socrates. The Republic is a dialogue — a written conversation — in which Socrates and several young Athenians argue about what justice is and whether it is worth having. Plato writes himself out of it; Socrates is the main speaker. Here the challenger is Glaucon, who builds the strongest possible case against justice precisely so that Socrates will have to answer it.
📚 PART 1 · THE CHALLENGE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Be honest with yourself: if you knew — with total certainty — that you could never be caught or seen, would you still do the right thing every time? (No wrong answer.)

The story is vivid — the storm, the chasm, the brass horse, the ring on the dead man’s finger — but it is built to deliver one hard claim. Once Gyges discovers he can become invisible, he uses the power to seduce, murder, and seize a kingdom. Then Glaucon presses the point: imagine giving one such ring to a just person and one to an unjust person. His prediction is blunt: “no man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice.” Both would end up acting the same way, because both would take what they wanted once the risk of getting caught was gone. From this Glaucon draws his real challenge: people are just “not willingly… but of necessity” — we behave only because we are afraid of consequences. Strip the consequences away, and justice goes with them.

📜 FROM THE TEXT · AS IT WAS WRITTEN
No man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature
that he would stand fast in justice.
📚 PART 2 · ARGUING THE SIDE YOU DON'T HOLD

Two things about how this works are worth noticing. First, the ring is a thought experiment — an imagined, impossible situation designed to test an idea by removing one factor (here, the chance of getting caught) and seeing what is left. Strip away consequences and ask: is there any reason left to be just? That is a tool philosophers and scientists both use, and you can use it in an argument of your own. Second, Glaucon does not actually believe injustice is better — he says so elsewhere. He is building the strongest possible case against his own view, on purpose, so that Socrates cannot win cheaply. That move — stating the opposing argument at full strength before answering it — is the mark of a serious thinker, and it is exactly what you practiced in your Week 3 Quick Write when you answered an objection.
