📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The Gita and the Bible spoke through story and image. The Analects work differently: short, plain sayings, collected by students who wanted to remember what their teacher said.
Confucius begins not with the cosmos but with something close to hand: learning, and looking honestly at yourself. Today’s sayings treat becoming a good person as a practice you work at, the way you would work at a skill. As you read, notice how much the sayings ask you to observe — others, and yourself.

▶  Read the passage — selected sayings from the Analects

The Master said: To learn, and to practise on occasion what one has learnt—is this not true pleasure? The coming of a friend from a far-off land—is this not true joy?

Make conscientiousness and sincerity your grand object. Have no friends not equal to yourself. If you have done wrong, be not ashamed to make amends.

Observe a man’s actions; scrutinise his motives; take note of the things that give him pleasure. How then can he hide from you what he really is?

The wise man will be slow to speak but quick to act.

From The Sayings of Confucius, translated by Lionel Giles (1907). Public domain.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Confucius (Kongzi, 551–479 BCE) lived in China during a time of political disorder. He was a teacher, and the Analects are not a book he wrote but a collection of his sayings and conversations, written down by his students after his death. That is why they are so short and so varied — they are remembered remarks, not a planned argument.
📚 PART 1 · GOODNESS AS A PRACTICE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of something you have actually gotten good at. How did you get there? (No wrong answer.)

Confucius opens not with a rule but with a question: “To learn, and to practise on occasion what one has learnt—is this not true pleasure?” The phrasing matters. A question invites you to agree from your own experience rather than simply obey, and it treats learning as a pleasure, not a chore. From there the sayings describe goodness as something you build by steady effort: make conscientiousness and sincerity your aim, keep company with people at least as good as you, and — the hard one — when you have done wrong, “be not ashamed to make amends.” Notice that none of this depends on where you were born or what you own. For Confucius, character is made, not inherited.

📜 FROM THE TEXT · AS IT WAS WRITTEN
Observe a man’s actions; scrutinise his motives; take note of the things that give him pleasure. How then can he hide from you what he really is?
📚 PART 2 · HOW A SAYING TEACHES

Look at how differently these short sayings work. One is a question (“is this not true pleasure?”). One is a string of commands (“Make conscientiousness and sincerity your grand object…”). One is a method with a payoff at the end: “Observe a man’s actions; scrutinise his motives; take note of the things that give him pleasure. How then can he hide from you what he really is?” That last one teaches by handing you a procedure and then showing what it reveals — that a person’s real character shows in what they do and enjoy, not in what they say. Held together, the sayings ask you to watch closely: to read other people by their actions, and to hold yourself to the same test.

💡 WHERE THIS CONNECTS

Set this beside the Gita and the Bible. The Gita located the good life in inner steadiness; the Sermon on the Mount in a reversal of who counts as blessed. Confucius locates it in something more ordinary and practical: daily effort, good company, and honest self-correction. Keep that difference in view — your project will ask you to weigh these answers against one another.
