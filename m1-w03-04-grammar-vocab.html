Five Words, and the Colon

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Five words from the Analects, and a punctuation mark you will use every time you introduce a quotation: the colon.
The Analects are full of colons — “The Master said:” and “the maxim of charity is such:”. Today you will learn what a colon does, how it differs from the semicolon you studied earlier, and how to use it to introduce a quotation cleanly — a move you need for every analytical paragraph you write.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define five words from the reading and use one in a sentence of my own.
2
use a colon correctly to introduce a list, an explanation, or a quotation, and tell it apart from a semicolon.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE DEFINITION
contentious (adj.)
eager to argue or quarrel. “The true gentleman is never contentious.”
conscientiousness (n.)
carefulness to do what is right; taking one’s duties seriously. “Make conscientiousness… your grand object.”
filial (adj.)
having to do with a son’s or daughter’s duty to a parent. “a truly filial son”
valour (n.)
courage, especially in facing danger. “the man of valour does not fear”
vacillate (v.)
to waver or go back and forth; to be unable to decide. “The man of wisdom does not vacillate.”

📚 PART 2 · THE COLON

A colon ( : ) says to the reader: here comes what I just promised. You put a complete sentence in front of it, and after it comes the thing that completes or proves the sentence — a list, an explanation, or a quotation. The Analects use it constantly to introduce the Master’s words: “The Master replied: Surely the maxim of charity is such…” That is exactly how you will use it most often in your own essays — to introduce a quotation after a full sentence of your own.

List: Confucius prizes three things: learning, good company, and honest self-correction.
Explanation: Confucius makes one demand above all: kindness toward others.
Quotation: Confucius gives a single rule to live by: “Do not unto others what you would not they should do unto you.”
📝 ON THE BOARD
Confucius gives a single rule to live by: “Do not unto others what you would not they should do unto you.”

Two things to get right. First, what comes before the colon must be a complete sentence. Write “Confucius prizes three things: …” (complete), not “Confucius prizes: …” (incomplete — “Confucius prizes” is not yet a full thought). Second, do not confuse the colon with the semicolon you learned earlier. A semicolon joins two complete, equal sentences; a colon points forward from a complete sentence to whatever follows from it.

💡 WHY THIS IS WORTH KNOWING

You now have three tools for joining ideas: the semicolon (two equal sentences), parallel structure (matching forms in a series), and the colon (a complete sentence pointing forward to what proves it). The colon especially earns its keep in analytical writing, where you constantly set up quotations. Use one to introduce your quotation in this week’s Quick Write.
