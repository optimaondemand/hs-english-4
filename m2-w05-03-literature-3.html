“I Am Alone”
The novel gives its last word to the monster — and asks you to judge
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Victor is dead. Walton finds the creature weeping over his maker’s body — and Shelley lets him speak last.
In the novel’s final pages, the creature grieves the man he hunted, condemns himself more harshly than anyone else could, demands that we see who else is to blame, and then vanishes into the dark to end his own life. It is one of the boldest choices in the book: the last voice we hear is not the scientist’s but the monster’s. Today — the last reading of the novel — you decide what to do with that voice, and pull the whole story together.

📖 THE READING

Walton discovers the creature beside Victor’s body. Read before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — the creature’s last words (the close)
His loneliness:

“… the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil. Yet even that enemy of God and man had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone.”

His demand for shared blame:

“… still I desired love and fellowship, and I was still spurned. Was there no injustice in this? Am I to be thought the only criminal, when all humankind sinned against me?… I, the miserable and the abandoned, am an abortion, to be spurned at, and kicked, and trampled on.”

His remorse:

“But it is true that I am a wretch. I have murdered the lovely and the helpless… You hate me, but your abhorrence cannot equal that with which I regard myself.”

The end:

He sprang from the cabin-window… upon the ice raft which lay close to the vessel. He was soon borne away by the waves and lost in darkness and distance.

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from the closing pages.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · AN ENDING THAT REFUSES TO JUDGE
Notice what Shelley does not do. She does not have a hero kill the monster. She does not redeem him, or damn him, or tell us what to think. The creature is not punished by anyone — he chooses his own end and disappears, “lost in darkness and distance.” The novel ends on an open question rather than a verdict. And the boldest choice of all: the last voice belongs to the creature, not the scientist, the explorer, or a narrator. By leaving us alone with the monster’s grief and refusing to render judgment for us, Shelley forces the reader to do what this course has trained you to do all year — weigh the evidence and reach your own considered conclusion.
📚 PART 1 · REMORSE AND ACCUSATION AT ONCE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
After everything — four murders, and a childhood of cruelty done to him — what do you feel toward the creature as he weeps over Victor’s body? (No wrong answer.)

The creature’s final speech does two things at once, and refuses to choose between them. First, real remorse: “it is true that I am a wretch. I have murdered the lovely and the helpless… your abhorrence cannot equal that with which I regard myself.” He does not excuse the killings; he hates himself for them more than we ever could. But second, a demand for shared blame: “Am I to be thought the only criminal, when all humankind sinned against me?” He insists we remember Felix who beat him, the world that spurned him, the maker who fled him — that he is “the miserable and the abandoned,” made a murderer by a lifetime of being “trampled on.” Both are true. He is guilty and wronged. The whole novel has trained you to hold exactly this kind of double truth without collapsing it into a simple label.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR VERDICT, AND THE WHOLE NOVEL

And then he is gone — not killed, not saved, just “lost in darkness and distance.” Shelley hands you the verdict she would not give. So gather the evidence you have been collecting for five weeks. He began “benevolent and good” (Week 3), chopping firewood for a poor family. He was abandoned at birth, beaten when he asked for kindness, denied the one companion that might have saved him (Week 4). He chose, in the end, to murder the innocent — William, Justine, Clerval, Elizabeth. And he ends in total isolation, hating himself, alone in a way even Satan was not. Is he a monster, a victim, or a mirror held up to the people — and the maker — who made him one? There is no single right answer; there is only the answer you can defend with the text. That defense is the heart of your module project, and the skill this whole year has built: not to repeat a verdict, but to reach one and stand behind it.

💡 WHERE THIS CONNECTS — THE WHOLE NOVEL

Every thread of the module meets in this final voice: origins (a being who never asked to be made), the creature/creator bond (a maker who fled what he made), the other (an outcast judged by his surface), and technology (power without responsibility). Next, we leave Geneva and the ice for a very different thinker — Nietzsche — who asks who gets to decide what counts as good and monstrous in the first place.
