Reading Himself in a Book
The creature finds three books — and his maker’s journal
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The creature finds a lost satchel of books and reads them “as a true history.” In one of them, he meets two figures who seem to be about him.
Among the books is Milton’s Paradise Lost, and the creature reads it looking for himself. He finds two mirrors: Adam, the beloved first creation, and Satan, the envious outcast — and he realizes he is built to be one but treated like the other. Then he discovers something worse: Victor’s own journal, recording the disgust with which he was made. Today is about how we come to understand who we are — and what it does to a person to learn they were never wanted.

▶  Read the passages — Paradise Lost, and the journal (Chapter 15)
Reading himself in Milton:

“But Paradise Lost excited different and far deeper emotions. I read it… as a true history… I often referred the several situations, as their similarity struck me, to my own. Like Adam, I was apparently united by no link to any other being in existence; but his state was far different from mine in every other respect. He had come forth from the hands of God a perfect creature, happy and prosperous, guarded by the especial care of his Creator… but I was wretched, helpless, and alone. Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition, for often, like him, when I viewed the bliss of my protectors, the bitter gall of envy rose within me.”

Reading his maker’s journal:

“It was your journal of the four months that preceded my creation… Everything is related in them which bears reference to my accursed origin… the minutest description of my odious and loathsome person is given, in language which painted your own horrors and rendered mine indelible. I sickened as I read. ‘Hateful day when I received life!’ I exclaimed in agony. ‘Accursed creator!’”

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapter 15.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
The three books the creature finds — Milton’s Paradise Lost, Plutarch’s Lives (noble figures of antiquity), and Goethe’s The Sorrows of Young Werter (intense feeling and despair) — were a real education in Shelley’s world: epic, history, and emotion. That he reads Paradise Lost “as a true history” tells us he has no one to explain that it is a poem; he takes its story of a Creator and his creatures literally, as the closest thing he has to an account of his own situation. Shelley quotes Milton on the novel’s title page (Adam’s lament to God: “Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay / To mould me Man?”), so this comparison is woven into the book from its first page.
📚 PART 1 · ADAM AND SATAN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever recognized yourself in a character — in a book, show, or song — and had it help you understand your own life? (No wrong answer.)

The creature reads Paradise Lost and measures himself against its two great figures. He is like Adam in one way: “united by no link to any other being in existence” — the first and only of his kind, with no parent, no partner, no people. But there the likeness ends. Adam “came forth from the hands of God a perfect creature, happy and prosperous, guarded by the especial care of his Creator.” The creature came forth and was abandoned. So he reaches for the other figure: “Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition” — not because he is evil, but because Satan, too, is cast out and made to watch others’ happiness while “the bitter gall of envy” rises in him. This is the creature’s tragedy stated in two names: he was made to be an Adam and treated as a Satan. He didn’t choose the second role. It was assigned to him by a creator who would not do an Adam-maker’s job.

📚 PART 2 · THE WORST BOOK OF ALL: HIS MAKER'S JOURNAL

Then the creature reads one more text — the papers he took from Victor’s coat the night he fled. It is Victor’s lab journal, “the journal of the four months that preceded my creation,” recording “every step” in “the minutest description of my odious and loathsome person… in language which painted your own horrors.” Imagine the cruelty of this for him: the one document in the world about his origin is a record of his maker’s revulsion at him. “I sickened as I read,” he says, and cries out, “Hateful day when I received life! Accursed creator!” In Part 1 he learned, from a poem, that he should have been loved like Adam. Here he learns, from his maker’s own hand, that he was hated from the first moment. Shelley is showing how a self is formed — and deformed — by the stories told about us. The creature’s slide toward rage is not a switch flipping; it is the slow accumulation of evidence that the world, and even his own creator, find him unforgivable for simply existing.
