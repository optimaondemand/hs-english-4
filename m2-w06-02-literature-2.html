“I Teach You the Superman”
Nietzsche’s answer to the question he opened — and what it is not
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If the old foundation of values is gone, Nietzsche says, then humans must reach for something higher than what they are now. He calls that something the “Superman.”
Before we start: this is not the comic-book Superman, and it is not a superior race — that’s a famous distortion we’ll take apart next lesson. The German word is Übermensch, sometimes translated “Overman” or “beyond-man.” It names an ideal of self-overcoming: a human being who creates their own values instead of merely inheriting them. Today we read what Nietzsche actually wrote, and weigh it.

▶  Read the passages — the Superman (Prologue)

“I teach you the Superman. Man is something that is to be surpassed. What have ye done to surpass man?… What is the ape to man? A laughing-stock, a thing of shame. And just the same shall man be to the Superman: a laughing-stock, a thing of shame.”

“The Superman is the meaning of the earth. Let your will say: The Superman SHALL BE the meaning of the earth!… I conjure you, my brethren, remain true to the earth, and believe not those who speak unto you of superearthly hopes!”

“Man is a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman—a rope over an abyss… What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not a goal: what is lovable in man is that he is an over-going and a down-going.”

Friedrich Nietzsche, Thus Spake Zarathustra, Prologue (Thomas Common translation). Public domain. “Superman” renders the German Übermensch.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · A WORD THAT TRANSLATES BADLY
“Superman” is a clumsy translation. The German Übermensch joins über (“over” or “beyond”) and Mensch (“human being”) — so “Overman” is closer. Nietzsche was not imagining a flying hero or a biological super-race; the comic-book Superman came decades later, and the racial misreading was forced onto Nietzsche after his death (we’ll examine that next lesson). His Übermensch is an ideal — a picture of a human being who has overcome their smaller self and created their own values rather than living by borrowed ones. Keep the English word in quotation marks in your head; the concept is about self-overcoming, not muscles or bloodlines.
📚 PART 1 · SOMETHING TO BE SURPASSED

Nietzsche’s starting claim is restless: “Man is something that is to be surpassed.” He doesn’t think human beings are a finished product; he thinks we are a stage, something meant to be exceeded. His comparison is deliberately humbling: “What is the ape to man? A laughing-stock… And just the same shall man be to the Superman.” Just as we look back at our animal origins, he says, a higher kind of human would look back at us. This is his answer to last lesson’s crisis. If values are no longer handed down from above, the task is not to despair but to overcome — to become the kind of person who can create worthy values rather than merely receive or reject them. The Superman is the name of that aspiration: not a person who exists, but a direction to grow in.

📚 PART 2 · A ROPE OVER AN ABYSS

The Prologue’s most famous image makes the idea vivid: “Man is a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman—a rope over an abyss.” We are not a safe, finished place to stand; we are a crossing — tense, dangerous, with a drop below. And then the line that reframes a human life: “What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not a goal.” Our worth, for Nietzsche, is not in arriving but in reaching across toward something higher. One more piece matters for reading him fairly: “remain true to the earth, and believe not those who speak… of superearthly hopes.” Nietzsche is insistently this-worldly. Where many of the Module 1 traditions located the highest good beyond this life, he plants it here, in this world, in what humans can become. You do not have to accept any of this. But notice how completely it answers the question he opened: with God removed as the source of values, the meaning of life becomes the hard, this-earthly work of surpassing yourself — a “dangerous crossing” with real risk of falling. Whether that is inspiring or alarming is exactly what next lesson asks you to judge.
