📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The freed prisoner has seen the sun. Now Plato asks the hardest question in the whole allegory: should he go back down into the cave?
Going back means re-entering the dark, looking foolish, and facing people who would rather not be disturbed — people who, Socrates says, might even kill anyone who tries to free them. As you read, ask why someone who has escaped would return at all, and notice the real-world shadow falling across the story.

📖 THE READING

▶  Read the passage — the return to the cave (Republic, Book VII)

Imagine once more… such an one coming suddenly out of the sun to be replaced in his old situation; would he not be certain to have his eyes full of darkness?

And if there were a contest, and he had to compete in measuring the shadows with the prisoners who had never moved out of the den, while his sight was still weak… would he not be ridiculous? Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes; and that it was better not even to think of ascending; and if any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death.

This entire allegory… you may now append… to the previous argument; the prison-house is the world of sight, the light of the fire is the sun… and… the journey upwards [is] the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world… in the world of knowledge the idea of good appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort…

[And] he who remembers this… will not be too ready to laugh; he will first ask whether that soul of man has come out of the brighter life, and is unable to see because unaccustomed to the dark, or having turned from darkness to the day is dazzled by excess of light.

Plato, Republic, Book VII. Public domain English text. Excerpt.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
The line about the prisoners putting the would-be liberator “to death” is not idle. Plato’s own teacher, Socrates — the speaker in this dialogue — was tried by the city of Athens and executed in 399 BCE, condemned in part for unsettling people with his questions. When Plato has Socrates describe a man killed for trying to free others from their illusions, the Athenian readers would have heard the echo at once. The allegory is also, quietly, about what Athens did to the wisest man Plato knew.
📚 PART 1 · WHY GO BACK?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If you understood something important that the people around you refused to see — and saying so would only get you mocked — would you keep quiet or speak up? (No wrong answer.)

Coming back down is bleak. The returning prisoner’s eyes are “full of darkness”; he can no longer compete at the one game the cave values — reading the shadows — and so he looks “ridiculous.” Worse, the others conclude “it was better not even to think of ascending,” and they turn hostile: anyone who tries to free them, they would “put to death.” So why return at all? The allegory’s logic is that the person who has seen the truth is not free to keep it to themselves. The light is not a private prize; having seen it lays an obligation on you toward the people still in the dark. That is the same question the Ring of Gyges raised in Lesson 6.1 — why do the hard, right thing when it costs you and no one rewards it? — now answered in a picture: you go back into the cave.

📚 PART 2 · PLATO READS HIS OWN ALLEGORY

Notice that Socrates then does something unusual: he hands you the key. “The prison-house is the world of sight, the light of the fire is the sun… the journey upwards [is] the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world.” An author decoding his own allegory is rare — usually a writer leaves the figure to work on its own — and it tells you how much weight Plato places on this image. The very top, “the idea of good,” he says “appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort.” And he closes with a piece of hard-won fairness: when you meet someone whose thinking seems confused, do not “be too ready to laugh.” First ask which way they are moving — stumbling because they have just come out of the light, or because they are being dazzled on the way into it. The same disorientation can mean opposite things. It is a quietly generous note on which to leave a story about how hard it is to see, and to help others see.
