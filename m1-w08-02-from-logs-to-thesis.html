From Seven Logs to One Claim
Turning your reading into an argument you can defend
🎯 OBJECTIVE
By the end of this lesson you will be able to write a thesis for the synthesis essay — a single arguable sentence that states your answer to “how ought a person to live?” and signals which traditions you will use to defend it.
⚡ WARM-UP · NO WRONG ANSWER
Finish this sentence in your head: “After reading all seven, I think the best life is mostly about ______.” What word or two did you reach for?
📚 WHAT “SYNTHESIS” MEANS HERE

A synthesis essay does not summarize seven texts one after another. That is a book report. Synthesis means you build your argument and pull the thinkers in as you need them — here two agree with you, there one challenges you, and you answer the challenge. The thinkers serve your claim; your claim does not serve them. Picture it as a conversation you are hosting: you have the floor, and you call on Aristotle or the Gita when they help you make a point or when you need to take one on.

So the first move is not “what did each text say?” (you already wrote that in your project document). The first move is: what do I think, and which texts back me up or push against me?

🧾 WHAT MAKES A THESIS STRONG

A workable thesis for this essay does three things at once: it takes a position a reasonable person could dispute, it names the ground (the traditions you’ll lean on), and it hints at the tension you’ll resolve. Compare:

TOO WEAK — not arguable
“Many ancient traditions had different ideas about how to live well.”
BETTER — takes a side, but vague
“I think Aristotle is right about how to live.”
STRONG — position + ground + tension
“The good life is built by habit, not handed to us by feeling or fate: Aristotle and Confucius are right that we become good by practice, and even the Gita’s call to act without attachment is, finally, a habit to be trained — which is why Glaucon’s claim that we are good only out of fear gets the human capacity for growth exactly backward.”

Notice the strong version names a claim (habit makes the good life), the ground (Aristotle, Confucius, the Gita), and the opponent it will answer (Glaucon). Yours can be shorter — one to three sentences — but it should do these jobs.
