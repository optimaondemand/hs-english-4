📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Genesis told you where the human being comes from. The Sermon on the Mount turns to how a person should live — and it begins by naming who is “blessed” in a way most listeners would not expect.
In the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus teaches a large crowd from a mountainside. The opening lines — the Beatitudes — are a list of who is “blessed,” followed by two short images, salt and light. As you read, watch the repeated sentence pattern, and notice which kinds of people the list calls blessed.

📚 PART 1 · WHO IS “BLESSED”?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think about who our culture usually treats as “winning” at life. What do they tend to have? (No wrong answer.)

The Beatitudes are built on one repeated sentence: “Blessed are [a kind of person]: for they shall [a promise].” Nine times the same frame returns, which makes the passage easy to hear and hard to forget. But look at who fills the first slot: the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek, the persecuted. These are not the people most societies call fortunate. The passage deliberately calls blessed exactly the people who seem to be losing — and attaches to each a promise that reverses their situation (those who mourn “shall be comforted”; the meek “shall inherit the earth”). The repeated structure is doing real work: it lines these reversals up one after another so the pattern itself becomes the argument.

📜 FROM THE TEXT · AS IT WAS WRITTEN
Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.
Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted.
Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth.
📚 PART 2 · SALT AND LIGHT

After the Beatitudes, the teaching shifts from who is blessed to what the listeners are to be, using two everyday images. “Ye are the salt of the earth” — salt preserves and gives flavor, but salt that has lost its savour is useless. “Ye are the light of the world” — and a light is meant to be set where it can be seen, not hidden under a basket. Both images make the same demand in different terms: what you are is only worth something if it actually does its work in the world. A good life, in this teaching, is not private; it is visible and useful to others.
