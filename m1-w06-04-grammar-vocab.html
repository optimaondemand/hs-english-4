Five Words, and Subordinate Clauses

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Five words from Plato, and the sentence tool that powers careful argument: the subordinate clause.
Plato’s arguments turn on words like if, because, although, and when — words that link one idea to another and show how they depend on each other. Today you’ll learn to build those links, and the one comma rule that goes with them.

2
use a subordinate clause to show a logical link (cause, contrast, condition) and punctuate it correctly.

📚 PART 2 · SUBORDINATE CLAUSES

A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb. An independent clause can stand alone as a sentence (“the prisoner was freed”). A subordinate (or dependent) clause cannot stand alone — it starts with a word like because, although, when, if, since, while, and it leans on a main clause to finish the thought (“because the prisoner was freed…” — you wait for the rest). These linking words are powerful because each one names a relationship: because = cause, although = contrast, if = condition, when = time. Argument runs on exactly these links. Watch Plato: “if any of them is liberated… he will suffer sharp pains” (condition), and “when he approaches the light his eyes will be dazzled” (time).

📝 ON THE BOARD
Because he had seen the sun, the freed prisoner pitied the others.
The comma rule. When the subordinate clause comes first, put a comma before the main clause:
— Because he had seen the sun, the freed prisoner pitied the others.  (comma)
When the main clause comes first, you usually need no comma:
— The freed prisoner pitied the others because he had seen the sun.  (no comma)

💡 WHY THIS IS WORTH KNOWING

Strong analytical writing is mostly subordinate clauses: because shows your reasoning, although handles the objection, if sets up a case. Short, choppy sentences can’t show how ideas connect; subordinate clauses can. In this week’s Quick Write — an argument about Glaucon’s challenge — build at least one sentence that opens with Although or Because, and punctuate it correctly.
