An innocent condemned — while the one who could save her stays silent
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A gentle servant girl, beloved by the family, is accused of William’s murder on a scrap of false evidence. Victor knows she is innocent. He says nothing that could save her.
Justine Moritz is tried, pressured into a false confession, and executed. Victor — the one person who knows the truth — stays quiet to protect his secret, and tortures himself in private instead. This chapter widens the novel’s question from “what does a creator owe his creation?” to a second one your Senior Studios work also asks: what do we owe the innocent people our choices put at risk?

▶  Read the passages — the false confession, and Victor’s guilt (Chapter 8)
Justine, to Elizabeth:

“I did confess, but I confessed a lie. I confessed, that I might obtain absolution; but now that falsehood lies heavier at my heart than all my other sins… Ever since I was condemned, my confessor has besieged me; he threatened and menaced, until I almost began to think that I was the monster that he said I was… I had none to support me; all looked on me as a wretch doomed to ignominy and perdition. What could I do? In an evil hour I subscribed to a lie; and now only am I truly miserable.”

Victor:

Thus the poor sufferer tried to comfort others and herself… But I, the true murderer, felt the never-dying worm alive in my bosom, which allowed of no hope or consolation… Justine died; she rested; and I was alive.

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapter 8.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
In the legal world Shelley knew, a confession was treated as the strongest possible proof of guilt — which gave authorities every reason to press hard for one. Justine, a poor servant with no family to defend her and little standing in court, is exactly the kind of person such a system could crush: condemned on thin circumstantial evidence (a portrait found in her pocket, which the creature had planted), then pressured by her own confessor into “confessing” with threats about her soul. Shelley, the daughter of the early feminist writer Mary Wollstonecraft, was alert to how the powerless — especially women and servants — fared when the powerful needed someone to blame. Justine’s fate is not just bad luck; it is a portrait of how easily the innocent and voiceless are sacrificed.
📚 PART 1 · A CONFESSION THAT ISN'T TRUE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Could you imagine confessing to something you didn’t do — if you were frightened, alone, and everyone already believed you were guilty? (No wrong answer.)

Read Justine’s explanation carefully, because it is not the confession of a guilty person — it is the anatomy of how an innocent one is broken. She confessed “that I might obtain absolution,” under a confessor who “threatened and menaced” her until she “almost began to think that I was the monster that he said I was.” That line is chilling: pressed hard enough, by everyone at once, she nearly believes the lie about herself. “I had none to support me,” she says — and there it is. Justine is destroyed not only by false evidence but by being alone, with no one willing to stand for her. The novel keeps returning to this: what happens to a person when no one will speak for them. It happened to the creature. Now it happens to Justine.

📚 PART 2 · THE SILENCE THAT KILLS

And one person could have spoken for her: Victor. He calls himself “the true murderer” — he knows that his creature did it, and that his silence is helping to hang an innocent woman. But he carries his guilt as private agony, “the never-dying worm alive in my bosom,” rather than as public confession. Here a careful reader has to hold two things at once. Victor genuinely suffers; his torment is real, and Shelley makes us feel it. And his suffering changes nothing for Justine — it is, in a way, the cheapest response available, because it costs him only feelings, not his reputation or safety. “Justine died; she rested; and I was alive.” The novel quietly indicts a kind of moral cowardice we all recognize: feeling terrible about a wrong while doing nothing that would actually cost us to stop it. Is private guilt worth anything if it never becomes action?
