📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Victor has agreed to build the companion. Half-finished, with the creature watching at the window, he stops — and destroys her.
For once, Victor thinks about consequences before he acts. He imagines what a second creature might do, and what the two together might unleash on the world — and tears his work to pieces. It may be the most responsible thing he does in the whole novel. It is also a brutal second abandonment, performed in front of the being who depended on it. Today you weigh whether breaking the promise was right, and watch the creature’s grief harden into a vow.

📖 THE READING

Victor is at work on the female creature when he stops to think. Read before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — the reasoning, the destruction, the vow (Chapter 20)
Victor reasons:

… one of the first results of those sympathies for which the dæmon thirsted would be children, and a race of devils would be propagated upon the earth who might make the very existence of the species of man a condition precarious and full of terror. Had I right, for my own benefit, to inflict this curse upon everlasting generations?… now, for the first time, the wickedness of my promise burst upon me…

The destruction:

… I thought with a sensation of madness on my promise of creating another like to him, and trembling with passion, tore to pieces the thing on which I was engaged. The wretch saw me destroy the creature on whose future existence he depended for happiness, and with a howl of devilish despair and revenge, withdrew.

The creature’s answer:

“Shall each man… find a wife for his bosom, and each beast have his mate, and I be alone?… revenge remains—revenge, henceforth dearer than light or food!… It is well. I go; but remember, I shall be with you on your wedding-night.”

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapter 20.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · A QUESTION AHEAD OF ITS TIME
Victor’s worry — that creating one new kind of being could lead to “a race of devils… propagated upon the earth” — is a remarkably modern thought. He is reasoning about unintended consequences at scale: not just what his creation will do, but what it will set in motion for “everlasting generations.” This is the same shape of question our own age asks about powerful technologies — once you release something self-replicating or self-deciding into the world, you cannot easily recall it. Shelley, writing in 1818, gives her scientist a flash of exactly the caution Victor lacked at the beginning. The tragedy is that he learns to ask “what could this unleash?” only after he has already made the first creature and broken its heart.
📚 PART 1 · THE CASE FOR DESTROYING HER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Is it ever right to break a promise — if keeping it might cause far greater harm? (No wrong answer.)

Give Victor his due: this is the first time in the novel he stops to think through consequences before acting. He realizes he knows nothing of the second creature’s disposition — she “might become ten thousand times more malignant” — and that the two together could produce “a race of devils… propagated upon the earth.” So he asks a genuinely serious moral question: “Had I right, for my own benefit, to inflict this curse upon everlasting generations?” To buy his own peace by risking all of humanity’s future would be, he decides, monstrous. Measured against his reckless behavior in the workshop, this is growth. He finally weighs the world beyond himself. By that light, tearing the creature “to pieces” is the responsible act of a maker who has, too late, learned to think.

📚 PART 2 · THE CASE AGAINST — AND THE VOW

Now read the same scene from the window. The creature “saw me destroy the creature on whose future existence he depended for happiness.” Victor does it in front of him — the one being who had pinned every hope on her. And notice the fairness problem buried in Victor’s reasoning: he condemns the female to non-existence for crimes she might commit, judging her exactly as the world judged the first creature — on what she is, before she has done anything. He even admits the female “might” be worse, or the two “might” hate each other. He destroys a possible life on a string of maybes. So which is it — prudent guardianship of the human future, or one more failure to keep faith with what he made? Shelley keeps both alive. What is not ambiguous is the result: the creature’s grief turns final. “Shall each man find a wife… and I be alone?” From here, “revenge” becomes “dearer than light or food,” and he leaves Victor with the sentence that will shape the rest of the book: “I shall be with you on your wedding-night.”
