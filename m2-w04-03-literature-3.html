📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The creature said he would take revenge on what Victor loves. He keeps his word — on Henry Clerval, Victor’s closest friend.
Shortly after the bargain is broken, Henry Clerval is found strangled, “the black mark of fingers on his neck.” Victor — who knows exactly whose hands made that mark, and yet again cannot say so — is even arrested for the crime. Today we watch the creature’s revenge turn outward onto the innocent, and see what Victor has truly lost: not just a friend, but the warm, humane life Clerval represented — the life Victor traded away for his ambition.

📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · CLERVAL AS FOIL
A foil is a character whose contrast lights up another character. Henry Clerval is Victor’s foil — and an image of the path Victor didn’t take. Where Victor buried himself in cold natural science and isolation, Clerval loved poetry, languages, people, and the “moral relations of things”; he is warmth, friendship, and a life lived among others. Earlier in the novel, Clerval nurses Victor back from the brink after the creation. So when the creature chooses Clerval as a target, Shelley is not killing a random character — she is destroying the very thing Victor abandoned to make his creature: human connection. The creature, denied all companionship, methodically strips Victor of his.
📚 PART 1 · REVENGE TURNS OUTWARD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When someone wants to hurt another person the most, why do they so often strike at the people that person loves rather than at them directly? (No wrong answer.)

The creature does not attack Victor. He attacks Victor’s world. The “black mark of fingers on his neck” is the creature’s signature — the same hands, the same method that killed William. And there is a terrible logic to the choice of victim. The creature was condemned to absolute loneliness; his revenge is to inflict that same loneliness on his maker, one loved one at a time. Notice, too, that Victor’s first words over the body are about himself: “Have my murderous machinations deprived you also… of life?” Even here, he understands that the death traces back to him. The chain that began with a single act of abandonment in the workshop has now reached Victor’s closest friend — and Victor, once again, cannot speak the truth that might explain it.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT VICTOR ACTUALLY LOST

To feel the full weight of this death, remember who Clerval was. He was the friend who loved “the moral relations of things,” who nursed Victor back to health, who stood for everything warm and human that Victor walked away from to pursue his cold experiment. In a real sense, Clerval was the life Victor could have lived. By killing him, the creature destroys not just a person but a possibility — the road back to an ordinary, connected, humane existence. This is the deep symmetry Shelley has been building toward: a creature made by the neglect of human bonds now devotes himself to severing every human bond his creator has left. Victor sacrificed connection for ambition; the creature, born of that sacrifice, completes it. Hold this as the novel rushes toward its end: the tragedy is not only what the creature does, but how exactly it mirrors what was done to him.
