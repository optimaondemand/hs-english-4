Lesson 3.2 · Literature
The True Gentleman
The second of three readings from the Analects this week
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Confucius’s ideal person — the “true gentleman” — has nothing to do with rank or birth. It is entirely a matter of character.
Today’s sayings describe what that person is like: courteous even in competition, steady under pressure, and unwilling to gain by doing wrong. Watch how often Confucius defines the gentleman by contrast — against the quarrelsome, the anxious, and the merely rich.

1
I will be able to describe what makes someone a “true gentleman” for Confucius, and what does not.
2
explain how Confucius uses contrast and a three-part parallel saying to define character.

📖 THE READING

▶  Read the passage — selected sayings on the gentleman

The Master said: The true gentleman is never contentious. If a spirit of rivalry is anywhere unavoidable, it is at a shooting-match. Yet even here he courteously salutes his opponents before taking up his position, and again when, having lost, he retires to drink the forfeit-cup. So that even when competing he remains a true gentleman.

In a state governed on right principles, poverty and low station are things to be ashamed of; in an ill-governed state, riches and rank are things to be ashamed of.

The man of wisdom does not vacillate; the man of natural goodness does not fret; the man of valour does not fear.

The man of knowledge finds pleasure in the sea, the man of virtue finds pleasure in the mountains. For the man of knowledge is restless and the man of virtue is calm.

From The Sayings of Confucius, translated by Lionel Giles (1907). Public domain.
📚 PART 1 · CHARACTER, NOT RANK
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you call someone “a class act,” what do you usually mean? (No wrong answer.)

“The true gentleman is never contentious.” Confucius then grants the one place rivalry is unavoidable — a shooting-match — and shows that even there the gentleman salutes his opponents and accepts losing gracefully. The example does the teaching: character shows not in avoiding competition but in how you compete. The next saying makes the point about status directly, and it cuts both ways: in a well-run state, being poor and obscure is shameful (you should have risen by good service); but in a corrupt state, being rich and high-ranking is shameful (you likely rose by going along with the corruption). Either way, wealth and rank are not the measure of a person. What matters is how the position was earned.

📚 PART 2 · THREE MARKS, IN PARALLEL

Look closely at this saying: “The man of wisdom does not vacillate; the man of natural goodness does not fret; the man of valour does not fear.” It is built on the parallel structure you studied last week — three matching clauses, each pairing a kind of person with the weakness they have overcome. The matching shape makes the three marks feel like one connected ideal rather than a random list: wisdom steadies the mind, goodness calms the heart, courage steadies the nerve. Confucius often defines this way, by laying qualities side by side in the same grammatical frame. When you meet a parallel saying like this, the parallelism is a clue that the parts are meant to be read together.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS

Notice the echo of the Gita: Confucius’s gentleman who “does not vacillate… does not fret… does not fear” is close cousin to the Gita’s steady, self-governed person. Two very different traditions arrive at a similar picture of an unshaken character. Differences like these — and the agreements — are exactly what your end-of-module project will weigh.
