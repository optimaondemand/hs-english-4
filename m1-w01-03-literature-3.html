📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Arjuna asks Krishna a direct question: what does a person who has reached this calm actually look like?
Krishna answers with a portrait of the steady, self-governed person, built from two comparisons — a tortoise and the ocean. As you read, pay attention to how each comparison works and to the chain Krishna traces from desire to the loss of self-control.

▶  Read the passage — The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter II (Arjuna’s question and Krishna’s reply)
Arjuna.
What is his mark who hath that steadfast heart,
Confirmed in holy meditation? How
Know we his speech, Kesava? Sits he, moves he
5Like other men?
Krishna.
When one, O Pritha's Son!
Abandoning desires which shake the mind—
Finds in his soul full comfort for his soul,
10He hath attained the Yog—that man is such!
In sorrows not dejected, and in joys
Not overjoyed; dwelling outside the stress
Of passion, fear, and anger; fixed in calms
Of lofty contemplation;—such an one
15Is Muni, is the Sage, the true Recluse!
He who to none and nowhere overbound
By ties of flesh, takes evil things and good
Neither desponding nor exulting, such
Bears wisdom's plainest mark! He who shall draw
20As the wise tortoise draws its four feet safe
Under its shield, his five frail senses back
Under the spirit's buckler from the world
Which else assails them, such an one, my Prince!
Hath wisdom's mark! Things that solicit sense
25Hold off from the self-governed; nay, it comes,
The appetites of him who lives beyond
Depart,—aroused no more. Yet may it chance,
O Son of Kunti! that a governed mind
Shall some time feel the sense-storms sweep, and wrest
30Strong self-control by the roots. Let him regain
His kingdom! let him conquer this, and sit
On Me intent. That man alone is wise
Who keeps the mastery of himself! If one
Ponders on objects of the sense, there springs
35Attraction; from attraction grows desire,
Desire flames to fierce passion, passion breeds
Recklessness; then the memory—all betrayed—
Lets noble purpose go, and saps the mind,
Till purpose, mind, and man are all undone.
40But, if one deals with objects of the sense
Not loving and not hating, making them
Serve his free soul, which rests serenely lord,
Lo! such a man comes to tranquillity;
And out of that tranquillity shall rise
45The end and healing of his earthly pains,
Since the will governed sets the soul at peace.
The soul of the ungoverned is not his,
Nor hath he knowledge of himself; which lacked,
How grows serenity? and, wanting that,
50Whence shall he hope for happiness?
The mind
That gives itself to follow shows of sense
Seeth its helm of wisdom rent away,
And, like a ship in waves of whirlwind, drives
55To wreck and death. Only with him, great Prince!
Whose senses are not swayed by things of sense—
Only with him who holds his mastery,
Shows wisdom perfect. What is midnight-gloom
To unenlightened souls shines wakeful day
60To his clear gaze; what seems as wakeful day
Is known for night, thick night of ignorance,
To his true-seeing eyes. Such is the Saint!
And like the ocean, day by day receiving
Floods from all lands, which never overflows
65Its boundary-line not leaping, and not leaving,
Fed by the rivers, but unswelled by those;—
So is the perfect one! to his soul's ocean
The world of sense pours streams of witchery;
They leave him as they find, without commotion,
70Taking their tribute, but remaining sea.
Yea! whoso, shaking off the yoke of flesh
Lives lord, not servant, of his lusts; set free
From pride, from passion, from the sin of "Self,"
Toucheth tranquillity! O Pritha's Son!
75That is the state of Brahm! There rests no dread
When that last step is reached! Live where he will,
Die when he may, such passeth from all 'plaining,
To blest Nirvana, with the Gods, attaining.

📚 PART 1 · A PORTRAIT, AND A TORTOISE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Krishna is about to describe a calm person who is not pulled around by desire. Before you read: does that sound appealing to you, or a little cold? (No wrong answer.)

After the teaching about action, Arjuna asks a practical question: what does a person who has actually reached this state look like? How do they speak, sit, and move? Krishna answers by describing someone who is not thrown off by sorrow or joy and who governs their own desires. His first comparison is to a tortoise.

📜 FROM THE TEXT · AS IT WAS WRITTEN
He who shall draw
As the wise tortoise draws its four feet safe
Under its shield, his five frail senses back
Under the spirit’s buckler from the world
Which else assails them, such an one, my Prince!
Hath wisdom’s mark!

The comparison makes a precise point. A tortoise can pull its limbs in under its shell when it needs to; a steady person can pull their attention back from whatever is pulling at the senses. Notice what the comparison does not say: the tortoise does not lose its legs, and the wise person does not destroy the senses. The senses are governed, not removed.

📚 PART 2 · A CHAIN, AND AN OCEAN

Krishna then describes what happens when a person does not govern their desires. He lays it out as a chain of steps: dwelling on something you want creates attraction; attraction grows into desire; desire becomes passion; passion leads to recklessness; and recklessness destroys judgment, “Till purpose, mind, and man are all undone.” Each step makes the next one more likely. He closes with a second comparison — this time to the ocean.

📖 FROM THE TEXT
And like the ocean, day by day receiving
   Floods from all lands, which never overflows
Its boundary-line not leaping, and not leaving,
   Fed by the rivers, but unswelled by those;—
So is the perfect one!

Rivers pour into the sea constantly, but the sea does not overflow or change its shape. Krishna’s point: experiences and desires keep arriving for a steady person too, but they are absorbed without throwing that person off balance. Put the two comparisons together and you have Krishna’s full picture — a person who can withdraw attention when needed (the tortoise) and who can take in whatever comes without being overwhelmed (the ocean).

💡 WHERE THIS FITS

This portrait is the Gita’s answer to the module’s central question: what does it mean to live well? Keep it in mind. When you read Aristotle in a few weeks, and the Stoic writer Marcus Aurelius later in the year, you will see related pictures of the calm, self-governed life — and you will be able to compare them. Your Quick Write this week, and your end-of-module project, will ask you to weigh these answers against each other and against your own.
