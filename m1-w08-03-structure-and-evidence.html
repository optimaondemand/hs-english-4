Lesson 8.3 · Structure & Evidence
Building the Body, Using the Quotes
How to organize the essay — and drop in evidence so it argues
🎯 OBJECTIVE
By the end of this lesson you will be able to outline the synthesis essay around your thesis and integrate quoted evidence using a signal phrase, the quotation, and your own explanation — so quotes carry the argument instead of just decorating it.
🧱 PART 1 · A SHAPE THAT FITS YOUR ARGUMENT

A synthesis essay is organized by ideas, not by texts. Do not write one paragraph per thinker. Instead, let each body paragraph advance one part of your thesis, calling on whichever thinkers serve it. A reliable shape for this essay:

Introduction — open with the question, then land your thesis.
Body 1 — your strongest reason, supported by the thinker(s) who back it.
Body 2 — a second reason, weaving in a different tradition.
Body 3 (counterargument) — the strongest view against you, stated fairly, then answered.
Conclusion — return to the question and say what your answer asks of a person.

That counterargument paragraph is the move you have rehearsed in every Quick Write since Week 3 — state the other side at full strength, then respond. It is what separates a real argument from a one-sided one.

📝 PART 2 · INTEGRATING A QUOTE: FRAME, QUOTE, EXPLAIN

A quote dropped in alone does no work. Surround it with three moves: a signal phrase that frames who is speaking and why, the quotation itself (introduced with a colon or a comma, per Lessons 7.4 and 6.4), and your explanation of how it supports your point. Watch the difference:

DROPPED IN — the quote floats
Habits matter a lot. “By doing just actions we come to be just.” So practice is important.
INTEGRATED — frame, quote, explain
Aristotle grounds character in repetition rather than nature: “by doing just actions we come to be just.” If virtue is built this way, then a person is never simply stuck being who they are — which is exactly why fear-of-getting-caught cannot be the whole story of why we act well.

The shaded parts are your words. As a rule of thumb, your explanation should be longer than the quote. The quote is the witness; you are the lawyer.

💬 QUOTING SACRED AND PHILOSOPHICAL TEXTS WITH CARE
Quote each tradition accurately and respectfully, in its own terms. Frame a religious text as that tradition’s teaching — “the Qur’an describes…,” “in the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus teaches…” — rather than as a claim every reader must share. You can argue with a tradition without misrepresenting it. Copy quotations exactly from your Learning Log or the lesson readings.
✏️ DO THIS NOW · IN YOUR PROJECT DOCUMENT
Under a heading Outline, write one sentence for each paragraph of your essay (intro, three bodies, conclusion), naming which thinker(s) each body paragraph will use. Then, under each body sentence, paste one exact quotation you plan to integrate there, copied from your Learning Log. You now have a skeleton with evidence already in place — drafting is filling it in.
