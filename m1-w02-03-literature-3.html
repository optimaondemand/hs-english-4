📚 PART 1 · AN ECHO OF GENESIS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When something is hard — a loss, a setback, a stretch of bad days — what actually helps you keep going? (No wrong answer.)

Read John’s first words and you should hear Genesis behind them. Genesis opens, “In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.” John opens, “In the beginning was the Word.” The echo is deliberate: John reaches back to the most familiar opening in the tradition and reuses it. But he also changes it. Where Genesis describes Yahweh speaking creation into being, John identifies the “Word” itself as God and as the one through whom “all things were made,” and then makes a claim Genesis never does: that this Word “was made flesh, and dwelt among us” — in Christian belief, that the Word became the man Jesus, called the Messiah. When a writer echoes an older, well-known text and then alters it, the change is where the meaning is. The echo says this belongs with Genesis; the alteration says here is something new.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT HOLDS UNDER PRESSURE

Psalm 23 and Romans 5 both answer the question of what a person can depend on, and both answer it through hardship rather than around it. Psalm 23 walks straight into “the valley of the shadow of death,” and its comfort is not that the danger disappears but that someone — Yahweh, the shepherd of the psalm — is present in it: “for thou art with me.” Romans 5 goes further and builds a chain, link by link: tribulation produces patience, patience produces experience, and experience produces hope. Notice the structure — it is the same step-by-step move you saw last week in the Gita, where desire led to attraction, then passion, then ruin. Here the chain runs the other direction: from suffering toward hope. Tracing a chain like this is a reading skill worth keeping; writers use it to argue that one thing reliably leads to another.
