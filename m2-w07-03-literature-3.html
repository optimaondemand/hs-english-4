Who Decides What Is Good?
Nietzsche against Shelley against a whole year of thinkers
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
This is the last reading of the module — and a place to gather the whole year’s argument into one question.
No new text today. Instead, you put the voices of this course in a room together: Nietzsche, Shelley, and the seven traditions of Module 1, all answering — differently — the question that has run beneath everything: where do our values come from, and who gets to decide what is good? The goal isn’t to crown a winner. It’s to see the conversation clearly enough to enter it with your own voice.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to contrast how Nietzsche, Shelley, and the Module 1 thinkers answer “who decides what is good?”
2
begin staking out my own answer, using the whole year as evidence.
📚 PART 1 · THREE ANSWERS IN ONE ROOM
⚡ WARM-UP · NO WRONG ANSWER
After a whole year, where do you currently think your own deepest values come from?

Line the answers up. The Module 1 traditions — the Gita, the Bible, Confucius, the Buddhist parables, the Qur’an, Plato, Aristotle — mostly agree on one move: the good is real and grounded in something larger than the individual — God, dharma, the cosmic order, human nature, eternal truth. Nietzsche denies exactly that: values have no such foundation; they were made by people, with histories and motives (Lesson 7.1), and once the old foundation weakens we must consciously create our own (Module 2, Week 6). Shelley sits between them as a story rather than an argument: Frankenstein shows what happens when a human being seizes a godlike power to create — and discovers, too late, that making something does not tell you what you owe it or what is good. Three answers to one question: the good is given (Module 1), the good is made (Nietzsche), and — Shelley’s warning — whoever makes it had better know what they’re doing.

📚 PART 2 · THE QUESTION COMES HOME TO YOU

Here is why this matters beyond a grade. You are, right now, a kind of creator — you proposed an app that will shape how other people live, and every week you’ve been asked not just “can I make this?” but “should I, and what makes it good?” That is the exact seam where this whole course meets your life. Nietzsche warns that you can’t simply assume the inherited answer; Shelley warns that creating without asking the question destroys what it touches; the Module 1 thinkers insist there is a real good to aim at. You don’t have to resolve a debate that has run for thousands of years. But you do have to take a position you can defend — in your project, and beyond it. Nietzsche himself said the mark of a serious person is to feel the pull of more than one side and still choose. So: gather your evidence, weigh the voices, and decide what you think the good is and where it comes from. That considered answer is the real product of English IV.

⚡ TRY IT · WHO SAYS WHAT?
Match the view to the thinker; you’ll get an instant note.
1. “Values were made by people and have a history; we must create our own.”
2. “The good is real and grounded in something larger than any individual.”
3. “Seizing the power to create without asking what you owe destroys what it touches.”
▶  Write it down: in your reading notes
Under a heading Lesson 7.3, answer in five or six sentences: Who decides what is good — is the good given, or made? Put Nietzsche in conversation with at least one Module 1 thinker and with Frankenstein, then state your own current answer. Quote one line. This is the seed of your final reflection and a strong spine for your project.
