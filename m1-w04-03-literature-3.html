Suffering, and the Scale of Grief
The last of three readings from the Buddhist parables this week
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The word Buddha means “the Awakened” — and what the tradition says he awakened to is suffering: its cause, and its cure.
Today’s short readings step back from the parables to the idea behind them. The Buddha describes the sheer scale of human grief in startling, almost impossible images. As you read, pay attention to how big the numbers and comparisons are — and ask why a teacher would describe suffering on that scale.

📖 THE READING

▶  Read the passages — the Buddha on suffering and grief

Buddha means Awakened or Enlightened, that is to say, awakened or enlightened to the cause and the cure of human suffering.

… in each of these lives every person has endured much suffering and misery.

Said the Buddha: “In weeping over the death of sons and daughters and other dear ones, every person, in the course of his past lives, has shed tears more abundant than all the water contained in the four great oceans.”

And again: “The bones left by a single person in the course of his past lives would form a pile so huge that were all the mountains to be gathered up and piled in a heap, that heap of mountains would appear as nothing beside it.”

From the introduction to The Grateful Elephant and Other Stories, translated from the Pali by Eugene Watson Burlingame (1923). Public domain.
📚 PART 1 · STARTING WITH SUFFERING
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Should a guide to living well start by facing how much suffering there is — or by focusing on what’s good? (No wrong answer.)

The whole tradition rests on a starting point named right in the word: the Buddha is “the Awakened,” awakened “to the cause and the cure of human suffering.” That phrasing is doing a lot. It frames suffering as the central problem of a human life — but it also frames it as something with a cure, something a person can wake up from rather than merely endure. Before the tradition offers any answer, it insists on facing the size of the problem honestly. That is what the next passages do, and they do it in a way you will not forget.

📚 PART 2 · OCEANS OF TEARS
📜 FROM THE TEXT · AS IT WAS WRITTEN
“In weeping over the death of sons and daughters and
other dear ones, every person, in the course of his
past lives, has shed tears more abundant than all the
water contained in the four great oceans.”

Look at the images: the tears one person has wept are “more abundant than all the water contained in the four great oceans”; the bones one person has left behind would dwarf a heap of all the mountains piled together. These are hyperbole — deliberate exaggeration, not meant as literal measurement. The question worth asking is why a teacher would reach for numbers this enormous. A small, accurate statement (“people suffer a lot”) slides right past us. An image of oceans of tears does not: it forces you to actually feel the scale of grief, your own and everyone else’s. And here is the turn — the goal is not despair. By making the sheer weight of suffering impossible to ignore, the passage is trying to shake the reader awake, toward compassion for everyone carrying it and toward the tradition’s search for a way out.
