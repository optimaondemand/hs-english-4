📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Last week Nietzsche asked who decides what is good. This week he gives a startling answer: our very words “good” and “evil” have a history — and it isn’t the one we assume.
In the Genealogy of Morals, Nietzsche acts like a detective tracing where moral words came from. His theory — “master” and “slave” morality — is one of the most provocative in philosophy. As always, our job is to understand it precisely and weigh it honestly. It is a theory about the origins of values, and you are free — even invited — to argue against it.

📖 THE READING

Nietzsche traces two opposite ways of creating values. Read before the lesson below; the older English is dense, so go slowly.

▶  Read the passages — master and slave morality (First Essay)
The powerful coin the word “good”:

… [the noble and high-minded] felt that they themselves were good… in contradistinction to all the low, the low-minded, the vulgar… It was out of this pathos of distance that they first arrogated the right to create values for their own profit, and to coin the names of such values… this is the origin of the antithesis of good and bad.

The powerless answer with a different value-set:

The revolt of the slaves in morals begins in the very principle of resentment becoming creative and giving birth to values… While every aristocratic morality springs from a triumphant affirmation of its own demands, the slave morality says “no” from the very outset to what is “outside itself,” “different from itself,” and “not itself”: and this “no” is its creative deed… its action is fundamentally a reaction.

The long quarrel:

… The two opposing values, “good and bad,” “good and evil,” have fought a dreadful, thousand-year fight in the world…

Friedrich Nietzsche, The Genealogy of Morals, First Essay (Horace B. Samuel translation). Public domain. Excerpts.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · WHAT A “GENEALOGY” IS
A genealogy traces a family’s descent — who came from whom. Nietzsche does this for ideas: instead of asking “what is good?” he asks “where did our idea of ‘good’ come from, and who first used the word, and why?” It’s a powerful and unsettling move, because it treats values not as eternal truths dropped from the sky but as things with a history — invented by particular people, in particular situations, for particular reasons. You don’t have to accept his answer to find the method illuminating: asking where an idea came from, and whose interests it served, is a tool you can use on any claim — including Nietzsche’s own.
📚 PART 1 · TWO WAYS TO MAKE A VALUE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Quick gut check: is “good” more about being strong, excellent, admirable — or about being kind, humble, harmless? (No wrong answer; notice which pull is stronger for you.)

Nietzsche claims there are two opposite ways human beings have created the word “good.” In what he calls master morality, the powerful and noble looked at themselves — strong, capable, flourishing — and called that “good,” with “bad” simply meaning low or common. They “arrogated the right to create values… and to coin the names of such values.” In other words, the strong defined “good” as a confident “yes” to their own kind of life. This is his blunt answer to last week’s question, who decides what is good? — historically, he says, the people with power did, by naming themselves the standard.

📚 PART 2 · THE REVOLT — AND THE OBJECTION

Then comes the “slave revolt in morals.” Those without power, Nietzsche argues, could not win by force, so they won by redefining the words: out of resentment, they declared the meek, the humble, and the harmless “good,” and the strong and proud “evil.” Where master morality begins with a “triumphant affirmation” of itself, slave morality “says ‘no’ from the very outset” to others — “its action is fundamentally a reaction.” And here is the genuinely uncomfortable part you should not dodge: the values Nietzsche files under “slave morality” — humility, meekness, compassion for the weak — are exactly the virtues most of us, and most of the Module 1 traditions, hold dearest. So the obvious, powerful objection writes itself: just because a value may have arisen from resentment does not make it false or bad. Compassion might be the noblest thing humans ever invented, whatever its origins. (This is the “genetic fallacy” — judging an idea by where it came from rather than whether it’s true.) Nietzsche wants to rattle your confidence that “good” is obvious and eternal. Whether he’s exposed a real history or smuggled in his own preference for the “strong” is exactly the argument a sharp reader has with him — and a perfect one for an essay.

💡 WHERE THIS CONNECTS

This is the deepest form of the module’s question. Frankenstein asked who gets to call a being a “monster”; Nietzsche asks who got to define “good” and “evil” in the first place — and answers that values were made by people, with motives. The Module 1 traditions would push back hard here, insisting the good is real and not merely invented. That clash is yours to judge.
