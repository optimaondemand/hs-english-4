The Blind Men and the Elephant
The second of three readings from the Buddhist parables this week
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday’s parable taught a virtue. Today’s teaches something about knowledge — and why people who are each partly right can still end up fighting.
A king has men who were blind from birth each touch a different part of an elephant, then asks each one what an elephant is. As you read, notice that no one in the story is lying — and yet they all end up in a brawl. The parable is interested in why.

▶  Read the parable — “The Blind Men and the Elephant”

In olden times, in this very city of Sāvatthi, there was a certain king. And that king ordered a certain man: “Come, my man, assemble in one place all the men in Sāvatthi who are blind from birth.” … And in obedience to the king’s command he let the blind men feel of the elephant.

Some of the blind men he let feel of the elephant’s head… Some of the elephant’s ears… Some of the tusks… Others he let feel of the trunk… others of the belly, others of the legs, others of the back, others of the tail, saying to each and to all: “This, O blind men, is what an elephant is like.”

Then that king… said this to those blind men: “Tell me, blind men, what is an elephant like?” The blind men who had felt of the elephant’s head, said: “Your majesty, an elephant is like a water-pot.” Those who had felt of the ears, said: “…like a winnowing-basket.” Those who had felt of the tusks: “…like a plow-share.” Those who had felt of the trunk: “…like a plow-pole.” Those of the belly: “…like a granary.” Those of the legs: “…like pillars.” Those of the back: “…like a mortar.” Those of the tail: “…like a fan.”

And they fought among themselves with their fists, saying: “This is what an elephant is like, that is not what an elephant is like;” “This is not what an elephant is like, that is what an elephant is like.” And thereat that king was delighted.

“Precisely so, O monks… they quarrel and brawl and wrangle and strike one another with the daggers of their tongues, saying: ‘This is right, that is not right;’ ‘This is not right, that is right.’”

From The Grateful Elephant and Other Stories, translated from the Pali by Eugene Watson Burlingame (1923). Public domain.
📚 PART 1 · EACH PARTLY RIGHT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of an argument where both sides were partly right but neither would budge. What kept it going? (No wrong answer.)
📜 FROM THE TEXT · AS IT WAS WRITTEN
“This, O blind men, is what an elephant is like.”

Here is the trap the parable sets: every blind man is telling the truth. The one who felt the ear really did feel something like a winnowing-basket; the one who felt a leg really did feel something like a pillar. None of them is lying or foolish. Their mistake is subtler and more human — each takes the one part he touched for the whole animal. The repeated line “This, O blind men, is what an elephant is like” quietly drives the point home: the same sentence is said over a head, an ear, a tusk, a tail. The words are identical; the thing each man means is completely different. That gap is where the quarrel comes from.

📚 PART 2 · A PICTURE FOR AN IDEA

At the end, the parable turns the picture into its point: people who “know not good… know not right” still “quarrel and brawl and wrangle and strike one another with the daggers of their tongues, saying: ‘This is right, that is not right.’” The elephant was never really about elephants. It is a concrete image standing in for an abstract idea: when people grasp only a piece of something large — a truth, a situation, another person — and mistake their piece for the whole, they fight. The genius of the parable is that you understand the abstract point (the danger of partial knowledge) through a scene you can picture instantly. Notice, too, the detail that the king is “delighted” by the brawl — a quiet warning about anyone who enjoys setting people against each other.

💡 WHERE THIS CONNECTS

This parable’s question — how much of the truth can any one person actually see? — comes back hard in a few weeks with Plato, whose prisoners in a cave mistake shadows for the whole of reality. Two traditions, far apart, both warning that we usually see only part. Hold the image; you will want it again.
