📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have almost certainly heard the phrase “God is dead.” You have probably also heard it misused. Today we read what Nietzsche actually meant by it — and why it is a diagnosis, not a celebration.
Friedrich Nietzsche puts the line in the mouth of a fictional prophet, Zarathustra, who comes down from a mountain to teach. Approaching this as readers, our job is not to agree or disagree with Nietzsche’s claim but to understand it precisely: what he was saying about his own time, and what hard question it forces. You can analyze his argument carefully whatever you yourself believe.

📖 THE READING

▶  Read the passage — the saint in the forest (Prologue)

The saint answered: “I make hymns and sing them; and in making hymns I laugh and weep and mumble: thus do I praise God… But what dost thou bring us as a gift?”

When Zarathustra had heard these words, he bowed to the saint and said: “What should I have to give thee! Let me rather hurry hence lest I take aught away from thee!”—And thus they parted from one another, the old man and Zarathustra, laughing like schoolboys.

When Zarathustra was alone, however, he said to his heart: “Could it be possible! This old saint in the forest hath not yet heard of it, that God is dead!”

Friedrich Nietzsche, Thus Spake Zarathustra, Prologue (Thomas Common translation). Public domain.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) was a German philosopher — and, notably, the son of a Lutheran pastor. He wrote in a Europe where the authority of organized religion was weakening under the pressure of science, industrialization, and historical scholarship. When he wrote “God is dead” (first in an earlier book, then here in Zarathustra, 1883–85), he was not claiming to have disproven God. He was describing what he saw as a fact about his culture: that belief in the Christian God no longer held European society together the way it once had. He gives the idea to a made-up prophet named after Zoroaster, the ancient Persian religious teacher, and writes in a deliberately grand, Biblical style — partly in earnest, partly to provoke. Read him as a diagnostician of his age, describing a change he found as alarming as it was momentous.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT THE LINE ACTUALLY CLAIMS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Where do you think most people get their sense of right and wrong — their bedrock of what’s good? (No wrong answer; we’re mapping the question, not settling it.)

Notice how Nietzsche stages the idea. Zarathustra meets a gentle old saint who still spends his days praising God — and Zarathustra does not argue with him or mock him; they part “laughing like schoolboys.” Only afterward, alone, does Zarathustra marvel: “This old saint… hath not yet heard of it, that God is dead!” The point of the staging is that the saint is a kind of holdover — sincere, but, in Nietzsche’s view, out of step with where his society has gone. So “God is dead” is best read as: the belief that once anchored our shared values has lost its grip on our civilization, whether or not people have noticed. It is a claim about a culture, not a proof about the universe — which is exactly why a person of any faith can study what Nietzsche is arguing without having to accept it.

📚 PART 2 · CRISIS AND OPENING

Here is the part that gets lost when the line is quoted as a slogan: Nietzsche found this development frightening, not delightful. If a whole civilization’s shared foundation for right and wrong weakens, two things can follow. One is nihilism — the despairing sense that nothing means anything, that no value is more true than any other. Nietzsche saw that danger clearly and dreaded it. But he also saw an opening: if values are no longer simply handed down, then human beings must take responsibility for creating their own — consciously, courageously, instead of drifting into nihilism. That is the project the rest of the Prologue sets up, and the subject of our next lesson. For now, hold the question, which is genuinely hard and which this whole course has circled: where do our values come from, and what happens to a person — or a society — that can no longer answer that easily?

💡 WHERE THIS CONNECTS

This is the bridge from Frankenstein. The novel asked who decides what counts as a monster; Nietzsche asks who decides what counts as good at all. He also stands in sharp contrast to the Module 1 thinkers — the Gita, the Bible, Confucius, the Qur’an, Plato, Aristotle — nearly all of whom grounded the good life in something beyond the individual self.
