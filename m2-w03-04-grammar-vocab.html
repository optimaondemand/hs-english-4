The Em Dash — the Most Useful Mark You Underuse
A punctuation mark for emphasis, interruption, and surprise
🎯 WHAT THIS LESSON IS FOR
Grammar resumes this week with a mark that works in every project format you might choose — essay, script, podcast, or journal. The em dash (—) lets you add emphasis, slip in an aside, or land a surprise. Shelley uses it constantly; so can you. Today you’ll learn its three main jobs and how not to overuse it.
✏️ PART 1 · GRAMMAR: THREE JOBS OF THE EM DASH

Notice it first. Here is Shelley, describing the moment of creation: “I beheld the wretch—the miserable monster whom I had created.” That dash makes you pause and brace before the naming. The em dash is a long dash (—), longer than a hyphen (-), and it does three jobs:

1. Emphasis — a single dash drives toward what follows:
“The creature wanted only one thing—to be loved.”
2. Interruption / an aside — a pair of dashes sets off extra information, like parentheses but louder:
“Victor—who had worked two years for this—fled the room.”
3. A sudden turn or surprise — a dash signals a break in thought:
“He reached out his hand—and Victor ran.”

Two cautions: when you set off an aside, use two dashes (one to open, one to close). And use the em dash sparingly — if every sentence has one, none of them land. In a Word document, typing two hyphens between words (word--word) usually auto-corrects to a true em dash.

📚 PART 2 · VOCABULARY FROM THE READING

Five words from this week’s chapters, in the sense they carry there.

benevolent (adjective)
kind; wishing to do good. “I was benevolent and good,” the creature insists.
consternation (noun)
alarm and dismay. The family’s “horror and consternation” on seeing him.
emblem (noun)
a symbol that stands for something. He calls Satan “the fitter emblem of my condition.”
loathsome (adjective)
causing disgust; hateful. Victor’s journal describes his “loathsome person.”
ardently (adverb)
with intense feeling or eagerness. He “ardently desired” to learn language.
transport (noun)
a powerful surge of emotion. Felix attacks “in a transport of fury.”
✏️ WRITTEN PRACTICE · COPIA

Start from this sentence built on the reading:

“The creature was benevolent at first, but consternation and cruelty turned him toward revenge.”

In your reading notes, rewrite this sentence two ways: (1) keep the meaning but use an em dash for emphasis or to set off an aside; (2) rewrite it about a different character or situation in the novel. Use at least two of this week’s vocabulary words across your sentences.

💡 CARRY IT INTO YOUR PROJECT

The em dash is a tool for voice — the pause before a hard truth, the aside that adds a second layer, the swerve that surprises. Whatever project format you chose on Friday, reach for it when you want a sentence (or a line of dialogue, or a narration) to land harder. Just don’t let it become a tic.
