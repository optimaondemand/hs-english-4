“I Ought to Be Thy Adam”
The creature finally speaks — and the story turns
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
High on a glacier in the Alps, Victor meets his creature face to face — and for the first time, the “monster” speaks.
What comes out is not a snarl. It is an argument — eloquent, reasoned, and devastating. The creature does not deny what he has done; he asks Victor to understand why, and to face what he, the creator, owes the thing he made and abandoned. This is the structural heart of the novel: Shelley hands the floor to the figure everyone has called a monster, and dares us to keep calling him that once we’ve heard him.

📖 THE READING

The creature stops Victor on the glacier and pleads to be heard. Read before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — the creature’s plea (Chapter 10)

“Be calm! I entreat you to hear me before you give vent to your hatred on my devoted head. Have I not suffered enough, that you seek to increase my misery?… I am thy creature, and I will be even mild and docile to my natural lord and king if thou wilt also perform thy part, the which thou owest me…

Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded. I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous.”

Victor, in response:

For the first time, also, I felt what the duties of a creator towards his creature were, and that I ought to render him happy before I complained of his wickedness.

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapter 10.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT · PARADISE LOST
The creature’s words lean on John Milton’s epic poem Paradise Lost (1667), which retells the Bible’s creation and the fall of Satan, and which the creature later teaches himself to read. Two figures from it matter here. Adam is the first human, made by a Creator who walks with him and provides for him. Satan is the “fallen angel,” cast out of heaven, excluded forever from joy. The creature’s tragedy is that he is built to be an Adam — a beloved first creation — but is treated like Satan, exiled by his maker “for no misdeed.” Shelley uses the comparison to put a hard question to the reader: if you make a being capable of love and goodness and then cast it out, which of you turns it into a devil?
📚 PART 1 · THE MONSTER ARGUES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Do you think people are born good or bad — or made that way by how they’re treated? (No wrong answer.)

The first shock is simply that the creature is articulate. The thing that could only make “inarticulate sounds” on the night of its birth now speaks in the careful, elevated language of a courtroom: “I entreat you to hear me before you give vent to your hatred.” He builds a real argument. He admits he has become dangerous, but he locates the cause: “I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend.” He did not begin evil — he was driven to it by total rejection. And he offers a bargain rooted in justice: “I am thy creature, and I will be… mild and docile… if thou wilt also perform thy part, the which thou owest me.” The creature is claiming that obligations run both ways between a maker and the thing it makes. He owes Victor obedience; Victor owes him care. Shelley has quietly turned the horror story into a debate about responsibility — and given the better argument, so far, to the monster.

📚 PART 2 · THE DUTIES OF A CREATOR

Then comes the line your whole module has been moving toward. Hearing the creature out, Victor admits: “For the first time… I felt what the duties of a creator towards his creature were, and that I ought to render him happy before I complained of his wickedness.” For the first time. He built a living being and never once, until this moment on the ice, asked what he owed it. The order of that sentence matters: he should make the creature happy before condemning its bad behavior, because the bad behavior grew out of the neglect. This is the moral the novel has been constructing from the first abandoned moment in the workshop — and it is, word for word, the question your Senior Studios work forces you to answer before you ask anyone to believe in what you make: what do I owe what I create, and whom will it affect? You don’t have to decide the creature is innocent. He kills; later he kills again. But Shelley insists you hold both truths at once — that the creature does terrible things, and that he was made terrible by a creator who would not do his part. That double vision, refusing the easy label of “monster,” is the most grown-up reading skill this novel teaches.
