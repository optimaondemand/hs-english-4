📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A novel about a man who makes a monster does not open with the monster. It opens with letters from a different man, sailing toward the North Pole.
Robert Walton is writing home to his sister as his ship pushes into the Arctic. He is chasing glory — a passage no one has found, a discovery that will make his name — and he is completely alone. Before we ever meet Victor Frankenstein, Shelley shows us a man with the same two things inside him: a burning ambition, and an ache for someone to share it with. Watch Walton closely; he is a warning the novel sets up before the danger arrives.

📖 THE READING

Robert Walton writes to his sister, Margaret Saville, in England. Read both passages before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — Walton’s letters (Letters 1–2)
From Letter 1 — the dream:

I try in vain to be persuaded that the pole is the seat of frost and desolation; it ever presents itself to my imagination as the region of beauty and delight… What may not be expected in a country of eternal light?… I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited, and may tread a land never before imprinted by the foot of man.

… you cannot contest the inestimable benefit which I shall confer on all mankind, to the last generation, by discovering a passage near the pole… This expedition has been the favourite dream of my early years.

From Letter 2 — the want:

But I have one want which I have never yet been able to satisfy… I have no friend, Margaret: when I am glowing with the enthusiasm of success, there will be none to participate my joy; if I am assailed by disappointment, no one will endeavour to sustain me in dejection… I desire the company of a man who could sympathise with me, whose eyes would reply to mine… I bitterly feel the want of a friend.

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Letters 1–2.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Mary Shelley began Frankenstein in 1816, when she was eighteen, during a cold, storm-darkened summer at a villa on Lake Geneva. Trapped indoors with the poets Lord Byron and Percy Shelley, the group challenged one another to write ghost stories. Hers grew into this novel, published in 1818. It is often called the first true work of science fiction, because its horror comes not from a ghost but from science — the new experiments of the age with electricity and the boundary between living and dead. Shelley tells it as an epistolary frame: a story inside letters. Walton’s letters wrap around Victor’s tale, which in turn will wrap around the creature’s. We never get the story raw; we always get it through someone.
📚 PART 1 · THE DREAM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of something you want badly enough that you’d sacrifice comfort, sleep, or safety for it. What is it? (No wrong answer.)

Walton is the picture of glorious ambition. He has talked himself out of seeing the pole as “frost and desolation” and into seeing “a country of eternal light.” He wants to go where no one has gone, to “satiate” his “ardent curiosity,” and — the part that should make us listen carefully — to win “the inestimable benefit which I shall confer on all mankind.” That is the language of a hero. It is also, almost exactly, the language Victor Frankenstein will use to justify creating life. Shelley is teaching us to hear it. Ambition that promises to benefit “all mankind” can be noble — or it can be a way of dressing up a hunger for glory as a gift to the world. Part of reading this novel well is learning to tell the two apart.

📚 PART 2 · THE WANT UNDERNEATH

Then, in the next letter, the bravado drops. “I have no friend, Margaret,” Walton admits. Surrounded by a hired crew, sailing toward the achievement of his life, he is starving for one thing his ambition cannot give him: someone “whose eyes would reply to mine.” This is the quiet engine of the whole book. Nearly every major figure in Frankenstein — Walton, Victor, and the creature most of all — is desperate for connection and ends up alone. Shelley pairs the two letters on purpose: the dream of standing where no one has stood, and the loneliness of having no one to stand beside. Hold that pairing. When the creature later begs Victor for a companion, you will recognize the same human want you met here, in the very first pages, in the mouth of the most “normal” character in the book.
