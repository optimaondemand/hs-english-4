📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Now we hear how the abandoned creature actually spent his first months alive — and it is nothing like a monster movie.
Hidden in a lean-to beside a poor cottage, the creature watches a family — the De Laceys — through a chink in the wall. He teaches himself their language, comes to love them, and, when he sees how little they have, secretly does their hardest chores at night. They never see him; they only find the work done and call it the help of a “good spirit.” Today’s reading is the evidence behind the creature’s claim from last week: “I was benevolent and good.”

📖 THE READING

The creature is telling Victor about his time watching the cottagers. Read before the lesson below.

▶  Read the passages — learning, and kindness (Chapters 12–13)
He gives up stealing their food:

“This trait of kindness moved me sensibly. I had been accustomed, during the night, to steal a part of their store for my own consumption, but when I found that in doing this I inflicted pain on the cottagers, I abstained, and satisfied myself with berries, nuts, and roots which I gathered from a neighbouring wood.”

He does their work in secret:

“… I found that the youth spent a great part of each day in collecting wood for the family fire; and during the night I often took his tools… and brought home firing sufficient for the consumption of several days… these labours, performed by an invisible hand, greatly astonished them; and once or twice I heard them, on these occasions, utter the words good spirit, wonderful…”

He longs to learn their language:

“I perceived that the words they spoke sometimes produced pleasure or pain, smiles or sadness, in the minds and countenances of the hearers. This was indeed a godlike science, and I ardently desired to become acquainted with it.”

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818). Public domain. Excerpts from Chapters 12–13.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Shelley was steeped in a great debate of her age about human nature. The philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau had argued that people are born essentially good and are corrupted by how society treats them — a direct challenge to the older idea that humans arrive already sinful. Shelley’s own parents were radical thinkers who believed character is shaped by education and environment. The creature is, in a sense, a living experiment in that question: a being with no past, no culture, and no upbringing, set loose to become whatever the world makes of him. Watch these chapters as that experiment’s opening data — what is he like before the world has finished with him?
📚 PART 1 · THE EVIDENCE OF A GENTLE START
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Picture the “Frankenstein monster” from movies or Halloween. Now imagine that figure quietly chopping firewood for a poor family in the dark. What’s the gap between the two? (No wrong answer.)

Look at the two acts of conscience in this reading. First, the creature has been quietly taking food from the cottagers at night — until he realizes they are poor and his theft “inflicted pain” on them. So he stops, and lives on “berries, nuts, and roots” instead. That is a moral choice: he gives up his own comfort the moment he understands it costs someone else. Second, seeing how hard the young man works gathering firewood, the creature secretly does the heavy labor at night, so the family wakes to find it done — the work of an “invisible hand” they bless as a “good spirit.” This is the being last week’s lesson promised: not born a fiend, but generous, self-sacrificing, hungry to be good. Shelley is building her case carefully, so that when the creature turns to violence later, we cannot say he was simply evil from the start.

📚 PART 2 · LEARNING TO BE HUMAN

The creature also does something deeply human: he wants to talk. He notices that words can produce “pleasure or pain, smiles or sadness” in others, and calls language a “godlike science” he “ardently desired” to learn. So he studies the cottagers’ speech month after month from his hiding place, learning to read and speak by watching a family he is too afraid to approach. Think about what this means for the novel’s argument. The creature acquires language, manners, and morality the same way every child does — by observing people who love each other and copying them. He is not programmed; he is raised, in a way, by a family that doesn’t know he exists. And that is the heartbreak underneath these gentle chapters: he is learning to belong to a human world that has no place for him, pressing his understanding against a wall he cannot cross. Everything good in him is real. So is the loneliness that comes with it.
