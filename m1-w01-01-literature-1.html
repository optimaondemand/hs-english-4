▶  Read the passage — The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter II (Krishna speaks)
Thou grievest where no grief should be! thou speak'st
Words lacking wisdom! for the wise in heart
Mourn not for those that live, nor those that die.
Nor I, nor thou, nor any one of these,
5Ever was not, nor ever will not be,
For ever and for ever afterwards.
All, that doth live, lives always! To man's frame
As there come infancy and youth and age,
So come there raisings-up and layings-down
10Of other and of other life-abodes,
Which the wise know, and fear not. This that irks—
Thy sense-life, thrilling to the elements—
Bringing thee heat and cold, sorrows and joys,
'Tis brief and mutable! Bear with it, Prince!
15As the wise bear. The soul which is not moved,
The soul that with a strong and constant calm
Takes sorrow and takes joy indifferently,
Lives in the life undying! That which is
Can never cease to be; that which is not
20Will not exist. To see this truth of both
Is theirs who part essence from accident,
Substance from shadow. Indestructible,
Learn thou! the Life is, spreading life through all;
It cannot anywhere, by any means,
25Be anywise diminished, stayed, or changed.
But for these fleeting frames which it informs
With spirit deathless, endless, infinite,
They perish. Let them perish, Prince! and fight!
He who shall say, "Lo! I have slain a man!"
30He who shall think, "Lo! I am slain!" those both
Know naught! Life cannot slay. Life is not slain!
Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never;
Never was time it was not; End and Beginning are dreams!
Birthless and deathless and changeless remaineth the spirit for ever;
35Death hath not touched it at all, dead though the house of it seems!
Who knoweth it exhaustless, self-sustained,
Immortal, indestructible,—shall such
Say, "I have killed a man, or caused to kill?"
Nay, but as when one layeth
40His worn-out robes away,
And taking new ones, sayeth,
"These will I wear to-day!"
So putteth by the spirit
Lightly its garb of flesh,
45And passeth to inherit
A residence afresh.
I say to thee weapons reach not the Life;
Flame burns it not, waters cannot o'erwhelm,
Nor dry winds wither it. Impenetrable,
50Unentered, unassailed, unharmed, untouched,
Immortal, all-arriving, stable, sure,
Invisible, ineffable, by word
And thought uncompassed, ever all itself,
Thus is the Soul declared! How wilt thou, then,—
55Knowing it so,—grieve when thou shouldst not grieve?
How, if thou hearest that the man new-dead
Is, like the man new-born, still living man—
One same, existent Spirit—wilt thou weep?
The end of birth is death; the end of death
60Is birth: this is ordained! and mournest thou,
Chief of the stalwart arm! for what befalls
Which could not otherwise befall? The birth
Of living things comes unperceived; the death
Comes unperceived; between them, beings perceive:
65What is there sorrowful herein, dear Prince?
Wonderful, wistful, to contemplate!
Difficult, doubtful, to speak upon!
Strange and great for tongue to relate,
Mystical hearing for every one!
70Nor wotteth man this, what a marvel it is,
When seeing, and saying, and hearing are done!
From The Song Celestial, or Bhagavad-Gîtâ, translated from the Sanskrit by Sir Edwin Arnold (1885). Public domain.
📚 PART 1 · A WARRIOR WHO WILL NOT FIGHT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of something you trained hard for with no promise it would pay off — a tryout, an audition, a test, a first real attempt at something that mattered. In the last moment before it began, what was loudest in your head? (No wrong answer — just check in with yourself.)

The Bhagavad Gita is set on a battlefield, just before a war begins. Arjuna, a warrior, asks his charioteer to drive into the space between the two armies so he can see who he will be fighting. He recognizes cousins, teachers, and friends among the opposing soldiers, and he decides he cannot fight them — he sets down his bow. His charioteer is Krishna, who in this poem is the god Vishnu in human form. Krishna answers him, and he does not begin with battle strategy or with reassurance. He begins with what Arjuna fears most, death, and makes a claim about it: the body can be killed, but the self that lives in the body cannot.

📖 FROM THE TEXT
Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never;
Never was time it was not; End and Beginning are dreams!
Birthless and deathless and changeless remaineth the spirit for ever;
Death hath not touched it at all, dead though the house of it seems!
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
The Gita is a 700-verse dialogue set inside the Mahabharata, the enormous epic of ancient India, composed and gathered over roughly 400 BCE to 400 CE. In the Hindu tradition, Krishna is an avatar — an earthly incarnation — of Vishnu, the great god who preserves and protects the world. So this scene is not a wise friend offering advice; it is a warrior being counseled by Vishnu himself, in the human form of Krishna, on the edge of battle. Knowing the Gita sits inside a much larger war epic also helps explain why Krishna and Arjuna come to be talking there in the first place.
📚 PART 2 · THE IMAGE THAT CARRIES THE IDEA

Krishna does not state the idea only in the abstract. He gives Arjuna a comparison: the soul changing bodies is like a person changing clothes.

📜 FROM THE TEXT · AS IT WAS WRITTEN
Nay, but as when one layeth
His worn-out robes away,
And taking new ones, sayeth,
“These will I wear to-day!”
So putteth by the spirit
Lightly its garb of flesh,
And passeth to inherit
A residence afresh.

A claim that is hard to picture — that the part of you that matters outlasts your body — is made easier to grasp by comparing death to an everyday action: taking off worn-out clothes and putting on new ones. The soul does not end; it moves to a new body. A few lines later, Krishna lists the soul’s qualities one after another — “Impenetrable, / Unentered, unassailed, unharmed, untouched, / Immortal, all-arriving, stable, sure, / Invisible, ineffable.” The repetition keeps the focus on one point: nothing can reach or harm it.

💪 WHERE THIS GOES

This scene introduces two threads the course returns to: a question about origins — what in a person does not begin or end — and the relationship between a creator and what it creates, here a person questioning God about duty and death. You will meet related questions in other texts this year. One thing to take into your own writing: Krishna makes a difficult idea easier to understand by tying it to a familiar comparison. That technique works in essays, not just poems.
