📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Last week’s text asked what in a person does not die. This week’s opens earlier still — with where everything, including the human being, comes from.
Genesis is the opening of the Bible and one of the most influential accounts of origins ever written. Today you read the creation of the world and of the first human. As you read, watch two things: the repeated pattern the writer uses to build the account, and the two very different things the text says a human being is.

📚 PART 1 · THE PATTERN OF THE DAYS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you hear the word create, what comes to mind first? (No wrong answer.)

Genesis is the opening of the Hebrew scriptures, and the God it describes — the creator worshipped in ancient Israel — is named Yahweh, rendered in this translation as “God” and “the LORD.” Read the first chapter and you will notice it is built out of a repeating pattern. Each day follows nearly the same steps: Yahweh speaks (“And God said”), the thing comes to be (“and it was so”), Yahweh evaluates it (“and God saw that it was good”), and the day closes (“the evening and the morning were the [first] day”). The effect of this repetition is order. The account is not a burst of chaos; it is measured, step by step, the same shape each time. That regular structure is part of the meaning — it presents creation as deliberate and good, not accidental.

📚 PART 2 · TWO THINGS A HUMAN BEING IS

When the account reaches the human being, it makes two claims that sit in tension. First, in 1:27, the human is made “in the image of God” and given dominion over the earth — a claim of remarkable dignity and authority. Then, in 2:7, the same creature is “formed of the dust of the ground” — made from the lowest material there is, and (as the text later says) destined to return to it. Hold both at once: the human being is, at the same moment, image-bearer and dust. Much of how later writers in this module and beyond think about human worth comes from trying to hold those two claims together.
